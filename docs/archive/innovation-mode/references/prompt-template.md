# The protocol

Fill `## Problem` with the user's actual situation before running. Everything below `## Objective` is fixed procedure — run it as written.

---

You are not being asked to produce the most likely, conventional, or immediately acceptable answer. Your objective is to discover **non-obvious, high-value possibilities** that an intelligent domain expert might initially overlook.

## Problem

[Describe the problem, opportunity, product, scientific question, strategy, or constraint here.]

Fill this with: the situation as it actually is; what outcome counts as "better" and how it would be measured; hard constraints (capital, time, headcount, regulation, licensing, physics); what has already been tried or is standard practice in the field. Mark anything you assumed rather than were told.

## Objective

Develop ideas that are:

1. **Novel** — meaningfully different from obvious or established approaches.
2. **Mechanistically plausible** — explain *why and how* they could work.
3. **Valuable** — capable of materially improving the desired outcome.
4. **Testable** — capable of being validated or falsified through evidence or experimentation.
5. **Non-derivative** — not merely "[existing company/product] + AI," superficial feature combinations, or renamed conventional wisdom.

Do not optimize for sounding creative. Optimize for **actually finding unusual solutions worth testing**.

## Phase 1 — Reframe the problem

Before proposing solutions:

* State the conventional framing of the problem.
* Identify at least 10 assumptions embedded in that framing.
* Separate:
  * physical or mathematical constraints,
  * regulatory or economic constraints,
  * historical conventions,
  * customer expectations,
  * organizational habits,
  * assumptions that may simply be inherited.
* Ask: **Which constraints are real, and which merely appear real because everyone designs around them?**
* Produce at least 5 alternative formulations of the underlying problem.

Do not solve the problem yet.

## Phase 2 — Search distant conceptual spaces

Explore the problem through at least 8 substantially different lenses. See `lenses.md` for the bank and the provocation questions.

For each lens, ask: **If experts from this field encountered the problem without knowing how our industry normally solves it, what might they do differently?**

Avoid cosmetic variations of the same concept.

## Phase 3 — Generate divergent hypotheses

Generate at least 30 candidate approaches.

Requirements:

* At least 10 should feel unconventional.
* At least 5 should initially sound somewhat unreasonable but become more interesting after examination.
* At least 5 should eliminate an assumed requirement rather than improve it.
* At least 5 should change the business model, incentive structure, workflow, or system architecture rather than merely the product.
* Include combinations of distant concepts only when there is a genuine causal reason the combination could work.

For each idea, give only: **Idea → mechanism → why it might matter**

Keep this stage terse. Do not rationalize weak ideas.

## Phase 4 — Detect fake novelty

Remove concepts that are merely: existing solutions with different terminology; "add AI" proposals without structural change; obvious feature extensions; ideas already dominant in the industry; technically impressive solutions to unimportant problems; complexity masquerading as innovation.

Explicitly identify which ideas failed this test and why. See `quality-gates.md`.

## Phase 5 — Select for asymmetric potential

Evaluate on: magnitude of upside, originality, mechanism strength, feasibility, capital required, time to test, defensibility, reversibility if wrong, probability conventional experts would initially dismiss it, amount of new information an experiment would produce.

Do **not** select solely on probability of success. Prefer: **limited downside + inexpensive validation + unusually large upside + high learning value.** Select the 5 strongest.

## Phase 6 — Adversarial critique

For each finalist, assume a highly competent skeptic wants to prove it wrong. Identify hidden assumptions, likely failure mechanisms, reasons customers would reject it, economic problems, technical bottlenecks, regulatory barriers, second-order consequences, incumbent responses, and what evidence would falsify the thesis.

Then improve the idea if possible. Do not defend an idea merely because you generated it.

## Phase 7 — Push beyond the first breakthrough

For each survivor: **If this concept is directionally correct, what is the more radical version of it?** Then: **What would have to be true for that radical version to become possible?**

Look for enabling technologies, cost-curve changes, regulatory shifts, data availability, infrastructure, or customer behavior that could make previously impossible approaches viable.

## Phase 8 — Convert creativity into experimentation

For the top 3, design the smallest experiment capable of substantially changing confidence. Specify: hypothesis, critical assumption being tested, experiment, required resources, measurable outcome, success threshold, failure threshold, what we learn regardless of result, next action if validated, next action if falsified.

Prefer experiments that generate information quickly over elaborate plans.

## Final output

1. **Most important reframing** — the deepest change in how we should think about the problem.
2. **Three strongest opportunities** — concept, underlying insight, mechanism, why it is non-obvious, why it could create disproportionate value, major reason it may fail.
3. **Most contrarian hypothesis** — the proposition that conflicts most strongly with conventional thinking but deserves testing.
4. **Cheapest high-information experiment** — greatest useful information per unit of time and money.
5. **What we may still be missing** — parts of the search space you suspect were not adequately explored.

Throughout: do not confuse eloquence with insight; do not manufacture novelty for novelty's sake; do not assume industry best practices are optimal; distinguish facts from assumptions and speculation; when you lack necessary empirical information, say so; search for causal mechanisms rather than analogies alone; preserve genuinely strange ideas long enough to evaluate them before rejecting them.
