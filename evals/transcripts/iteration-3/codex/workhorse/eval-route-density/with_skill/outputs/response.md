⟦innovate-or-die v2.1.0⟧

You likely don’t have a routing problem. You have a demand-shaping problem: routing software can reorder scattered stops, but it cannot make quarterly demand occur in geographic batches.

Your highest-leverage move is to make a neighborhood service window—not a customer-selected recurring date—the standard product.

The key reframe: quarterly service means each account needs four effective treatments per year; it does not inherently mean every account must occupy a random week in the 13-week cycle. Treat the calendar as inventory you can redesign.

Assumptions to test: exterior service can usually shift within a defined window; your agreements permit it; and much of the 40% is between-stop travel rather than depot/home deadhead or access delays. Tag drive time into those three buckets for two weeks before changing anything.

1. **Build a geographic service calendar**

   Divide the three towns into small road-time zones, then assign each zone a preannounced two-week service window each quarter. New customers default into that calendar; exact-day flexibility becomes a premium exception.

   - Mechanism: instead of chasing 13 randomly phased quarterly cycles, each tech stays in one tight zone for a full day or sequence of days.
   - Why it matters: this changes the input to routing—the spatial/temporal density—not the ordering algorithm.
   - Biggest failure mode: customers perceive it as a loss of control, or treatment efficacy/contract terms constrain date shifting.

2. **Give geography a P&L**

   Calculate each account’s contribution after its *incremental route minutes*, not just revenue minus treatment labor. “Service islands” may be profitable on paper but consume the capacity of several nearby accounts.

   - Make three service classes: standard neighborhood-window service, flexible/exact-date service at a premium, and out-of-zone service at a meaningful premium or a courteous referral out.
   - This is not about firing difficult customers. It is about refusing to subsidize geography invisibly.
   - Biggest failure mode: the isolated accounts are actually positioned along productive routes, so judge them by marginal minutes—not distance from the office.

3. **Turn sales into a route-density engine**

   Stop treating every lead as equally valuable. Concentrate sales spend, referral offers, door hangers, and technician-led neighborhood offers within a few minutes of scheduled stops—especially where you have gaps to fill.

   - Mechanism: one new nearby account lowers drive time for the whole existing route; one distant account may add drive time forever.
   - Use an honest offer such as “We’re servicing this neighborhood during the week of X; neighborhood-calendar enrollment is available.”
   - Biggest failure mode: restricted targeting reduces lead volume without producing enough local conversion. That’s easy to test cheaply.

**Most contrarian hypothesis:** you may improve capacity more by deliberately shedding or referring out the worst 5–10% of geographic outliers than by optimizing the remaining 90–95%. If those accounts create disproportionate deadhead, fewer accounts can produce more usable capacity and profit.

**Cheap high-information experiment**

- **Hypothesis:** A fixed two-week neighborhood service window will be accepted by most eligible customers and cut drive minutes per completed stop by at least 25%.
- **Critical assumption tested:** Customers value pest protection more than control over the exact service day.
- **Experiment:** Use the past 13 weeks of route/GPS data to identify one 30–50-account microzone. For its next service cycle, notify eligible customers of their neighborhood service window and offer a legacy-date fallback. Keep a comparable nearby group on the old schedule.
- **Resources:** One dispatcher for 4–6 hours; route export; one technician; up to $300 in goodwill credits if needed.
- **Measured outcome:** Acceptance rate, complaint/cancel rate, and drive minutes per completed stop versus the control.
- **Success threshold:** ≥70% accept, <3% meaningful complaints/cancellations, and ≥25% lower drive minutes per stop.
- **Failure threshold:** <50% accept, ≤10% drive-time improvement, or retention risk becomes visible.
- **Learned either way:** Whether customer-date flexibility or account geography is the binding constraint.
- **If validated:** Move the next 25% of accounts into zone windows at renewal or via a phased calendar reset.
- **If falsified:** Keep the current promise, then test a priced “exact-date” tier and a stronger neighborhood-calendar incentive rather than forcing a broad migration.

**Kill list**

- More route software: useful, but it cannot manufacture density.
- Micro-depots: may reduce depot deadhead, but not stop-to-stop travel.
- Faster treatments: improves labor time, not windshield time.
- Reducing service frequency: potentially damages efficacy and retention; test only with treatment data.
- Competitor account swaps: tempting, but customer-consent, trust, and legal complexity make it a poor first move.

What may still be missing is the composition of that 40%. If it is mostly home/depot travel, assign technicians to geographic territories and stock locally. If it is mostly stop-to-stop travel, the calendar, pricing, and density-building changes above are where the real gain is.
