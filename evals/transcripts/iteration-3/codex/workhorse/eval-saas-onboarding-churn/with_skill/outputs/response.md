⟦innovate-or-die v2.1.0⟧

The strongest thesis: treat early churn as a **failed project activation**, not a failed onboarding experience. A subcontractor should not be “onboarded” until the product is producing a decision or financial outcome on one named, live job.

The 45-minute call and docs assume the customer’s scarce resource is knowledge. More likely, it is a project manager’s attention, access to live job data, and confidence that changing a workflow will not create risk. Improving training may help, but it will not fix customers who never attach the product to a consequential project.

The highest-leverage moves:

1. **Replace account onboarding with an “active-job launch.”**  
   On the sale or kickoff call, select one active project, one accountable project lead, one recurring decision, and one measurable pain—e.g., missed change-order evidence, pay-app status, labor-cost variance, or compliance exceptions. Configure and run that narrow workflow until it produces a weekly action.

   Mechanism: it creates an irreversible operational dependency before the first renewal decision, rather than measuring superficial activity such as logins or completed setup.

   Architecture implication: make the core entity a project workspace with roles, imported artifacts, event ingestion, and an “exceptions/actions” output—not a generic company account.

   Biggest risk: some customers genuinely lack a suitable live project; that is valuable qualification data rather than a reason to force adoption.

2. **Turn a narrow workflow into a managed, outcome-priced service.**  
   For a high-value use case, offer: “We run this with you for the first 90 days; you pay per active project or from verified recovered/accelerated value.” Start with one workflow where value can be observed, such as change-order capture or payment/compliance follow-up.

   Mechanism: the vendor absorbs the adoption risk while the customer sees an outcome before being asked to sustain a new system. It also aligns the economic buyer with the people entering or approving information.

   Why it is disproportionate: a managed launch can expose whether churn comes from bad fit, weak product value, insufficient capacity, or pricing friction—much faster than redesigning onboarding screens.

   Biggest risk: service cost can destroy margins. Bound the scope tightly, standardize data intake, and stop if a customer cannot provide the named project data and owner.

3. **Make the product enter through the project ecosystem, not the subcontractor’s back office.**  
   Sell or distribute through GCs, project-management platforms, payroll/material partners, or trade-specific associations. The product arrives with a job, participants, documents, and a reason to act—not an empty account that a subcontractor must populate.

   Mechanism: the activation asset is pre-existing project context and coordination pressure. This eliminates much of the setup burden and can create a network advantage as counterparties share the same project workflow.

   Biggest risk: enterprise/channel sales cycles and integration dependence. Begin with a sponsored pilot with one GC and a small cohort of subs; do not build a broad marketplace first.

The most contrarian hypothesis worth testing: **a meaningful fraction of early churn is desirable signal, not an onboarding defect.** The company may be selling before a customer has a live project, usable data, a named operator, or a pain severe enough to justify change. Tightening “launch readiness” could initially reduce new-logo volume while improving retention, sales efficiency, and customer-success capacity.

Use a simple launch-readiness gate before closing or provisioning:

- Named active project within 30 days
- Named operating owner, not only executive sponsor
- One target outcome and baseline
- Access to the minimum source documents/system
- Commitment to a 20-minute weekly operating review for 30 days

Track cohort health by `days to named project`, `days to first source data`, `days to first cross-role action`, and `days to first documented decision/outcome`—not tutorial completion.

Cheapest high-information experiment:

```text
Hypothesis: Requiring a named live project and delivering one weekly operational outcome
will materially reduce 90-day churn versus normal onboarding.

Critical assumption tested: Customers will give access and operating-owner time when the
offer is tied to a live project and a concrete outcome.

Experiment: Take 12 new or recently churned accounts. Offer a 30-day “active-job launch”:
the team configures one narrow workflow from their real project artifacts and sends one
weekly action report. No broad training or portal requirement.

Resources: One implementation/success operator; 2–4 hours per customer setup; a weekly
20-minute customer review; existing product plus manual concierge work.

Measured outcome: Share providing project data within five business days; share completing
four weekly reviews; share with a documented action or financial-risk finding by day 30;
90-day retained share.

Success threshold: At least 8 of 12 provide data, 6 complete the four-week cadence, and
4 generate a documented action/outcome. Then make active-job launch the default motion.

Failure threshold: Fewer than 4 provide usable data or fewer than 3 sustain the cadence.
Stop treating onboarding as the primary lever; focus on ICP, sales qualification, or the
workflow’s underlying value proposition.

Learned either way: Whether the binding constraint is customer motivation/data access,
implementation capacity, or product value.

If validated: Productize project templates, data connectors, exception queues, and a
launch-readiness sales gate.

If falsified: Interview the nonparticipants around why the live project was unavailable
or not worth connecting, then narrow the ICP or redesign the commercial promise.
```

Compact kill list:

- More documentation, webinars, and nurture emails — useful hygiene, but cannot create a live operational dependency.
- Generic AI summaries — an efficiency feature unless it changes who owns the workflow and when action happens.
- Gamified setup checklists — measures product interaction, not construction value.
- Blanket price discounts — may postpone cancellation while worsening customer quality.
- Building a broad subcontractor marketplace first — high coordination cost before proving one shared-project use case.

What may still be missing: the dominant churn driver could be sales-source mismatch, a trade-specific workflow gap, a failed integration, or construction seasonality/cash stress. Segment every early cancellation by trade, company size, acquisition source, presence of an active job at sale, integration status, and first-outcome timing before committing to any one remedy.
