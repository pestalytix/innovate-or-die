⟦innovate-or-die v2.1.0⟧

I wouldn’t drop author events entirely. I’d stop running them as a default free retail activity and put a 90-day pause on store-funded public events. Keep only events that are prepaid or underwritten, then decide with fully loaded numbers—not same-night sales.

The key reframe: staff energy is the scarce asset, not chairs. Treat commercial events as products that must earn their labor; treat the occasional community event as an explicit, capped cultural investment—not a “break-even” fiction.

Two opportunities worth testing:

1. **Institution-backed author stops.** Approach schools, libraries, nonprofits, and workplaces: they provide venue, audience, and a firm book order or fee; you handle curation, books, and fulfillment. The value is that the partner already has an audience and budget, so you stop producing a night from scratch. It fails if partners like the idea but will not commit money or book orders before scheduling.

2. **Small, pre-committed reader cohorts.** Replace a broad event with 12–15 readers who buy the book first, meet around it, then get a short author encounter. It works because the purchase happens before staff labor and the encounter deepens repeat-reader relationships. It fails if it merely concentrates your existing loyal customers without increasing their spend or retention.

My most contrarian hypothesis: the public event is not the valuable part. Your real asset may be trusted reader relationships and local fulfillment; events are just one expensive way to activate them.

Run this cheapest high-information test first:

- **Hypothesis:** Your typical public event does not generate enough direct and 90-day incremental gross profit to cover its full staff cost.
- **Critical assumption:** You can reasonably compare attendees with similar non-attendees.
- **Experiment:** Audit your last six events. Include every paid staff hour—planning, setup, hosting, cleanup, follow-up—at a loaded hourly cost, cash expenses, and lost normal retail contribution. For attendees identifiable in your POS/email list, compare their 90-day gross profit before vs. after the event against a matched group with similar prior purchase frequency/spend. Treat new-to-store attendees separately, comparing them with other new customers acquired that month.
- **Resources:** About four manager hours and POS/email exports; no new spend.
- **Measured outcome:** Estimated incremental 90-day gross profit and contribution per staff hour for each event.
- **Success threshold:** The median event covers its fully loaded cost after estimated 90-day lift.
- **Failure threshold:** The median event fails either measure. End routine public events for the next season.
- **Learned either way:** Whether events are genuinely acquisition/retention investments or subsidized entertainment.
- **If validated:** Keep only the formats and authors that clear the threshold; test the reader-cohort version.
- **If falsified:** Retain only prepaid, partner-funded, or explicitly mission-funded events.

In parallel, email 15 qualified institutions with one concrete offer: “You host and recruit; we supply books and author programming; a 25-book commitment or fee is required before booking.” Three written commitments is a strong signal to build the model; zero or one means don’t invest further.

For the reader-cohort test, offer 12 slots to a clearly defined genre/customer segment for one upcoming author. Stop if fewer than 12 books are precommitted within a week, or if contribution per staff hour isn’t at least 1.5× your recent-event median.

A few ideas I’d reject for now:

- Charging admission or taking refundable deposits: useful controls, not a strategy.
- Arbitrary preorder thresholds: they prevent losses but don’t create demand.
- Better check-in/templates: operational hygiene, not a reason to keep events.
- Offsite partners without a guaranteed audience or order: often just moves the labor around.
- Signed bookplates alone: likely cannibalize sales rather than create new value.

During the pause, honor existing commitments and tell core publishers/partners it’s an operating-model review, not a retreat from authors. Revisit early if two of your most important relationships say the change materially affects working with you.

The biggest unknown is what “barely break even” excludes: staff time, disrupted retail, and whether attendees become better customers later. Get that answer before making a permanent all-or-nothing call.
