You’re probably optimizing the wrong layer. Routing software can sequence dispersed appointments; it cannot fix a business that sells 900 separate promises across a wide geography.

The missing lever is **demand shaping**: make route density part of the product and contract, not a dispatch problem left for the day before service.

The important reframing: quarterly service does not necessarily mean each customer needs their own preferred service date. If your contracts and local treatment/notice rules allow it, sell a default “neighborhood service window” (for example, a company-selected 2–4 week window within the quarter), with a priced exception for exact dates or special-access needs. That lets you deliberately create dense service cells.

Your best opportunities:

1. **Neighborhood-default membership**

   Make flexible, company-set service windows the standard plan at renewal; exact-date/access coordination becomes a premium exception. The mechanism is simple: customers who value price/reliability over date control give dispatch the ability to synchronize a street or micro-area. This is packaging, not software—and it could materially reduce inter-stop gaps.

   It fails if gated homes, pets, work-from-home customers, or anxiety about treatment mean most customers truly need specific coordination.

2. **Manage the book as a geographic portfolio**

   Identify accounts that create route tails, then calculate their *marginal* drive cost—not their standalone revenue. Refer, transfer, or decline the worst tail accounts while concentrating sales only in cells you already serve. A smaller book can produce more completed services per paid hour.

   It fails if the “tail” accounts are not actually causing much driving, or removing them collapses otherwise viable routes. Measure this before dropping anyone.

3. **Sell bounded-area aggregation**

   Treat an HOA, property manager, or a genuinely coordinated street as one commercial unit: one service day, centralized enrollment/access where possible, multiple homes. This is slower to sell, but it can change the economics far more than squeezing another 5% from routing.

   It fails if pest control is too private for neighbors to coordinate, or the buyer’s approval cycle is too long.

The contrarian hypothesis worth testing: **your ideal customer is not the most profitable individual account; it is the account that completes a service cell.** A neighbor account may be worth far more operationally than an isolated high-revenue account.

Before changing anything, split the current 40% windshield time into: depot deadhead, travel between stops, appointment-window waiting, failed-access stops, replenishment returns, and sparse-tail mileage. Each has a different answer. If most of it is daily depot travel, your problem is deployment, not customer scheduling.

Run this cheapest useful test in one dense micro-area over the next 4–6 weeks:

```text
Hypothesis: At least 35% of eligible renewal customers will accept a
company-selected 2–4 week neighborhood service window, and the resulting
cohort will cut paid drive minutes per completed service by at least 20%.

Critical assumption tested: Customers value quarterly pest coverage more than
control over the exact service date.

Experiment: Pick one cell with 50–80 accounts due in the next 60 days. Offer
renewing customers the flexible neighborhood plan; keep a matched nearby
group on current scheduling. Do not delay any required service.

Resources: Existing CRM, a map export, 4–6 hours of dispatch analysis, and
calls/texts from the office; no new software or hires.

Measured outcome: Acceptance rate; drive minutes/completed job; cancellations,
complaints, access failures, discount cost, and added office time.

Success threshold: ≥35% acceptance, ≥20% lower drive minutes/job, and no more
than a 3-point increase in complaints/cancellations versus control.

Failure threshold: <20% acceptance or no meaningful reduction in drive time.

Learned either way: Whether scheduling fragmentation—not just sparse
geography—is the binding constraint.

If validated: Convert the offer into the default renewal product and expand
cell by cell.

If falsified: Stop trying to shift customers; focus on portfolio pruning,
territory launches, and deployment/depot analysis instead.
```

Kill list:

- Remote/photo-based replacement for quarterly visits: don’t pursue until you separate legal, label, contract, and efficacy requirements.
- Holding work until a route “fills”: risks overdue service and churn.
- Tech density bonuses: likely to create rushed work and avoidance of hard accounts.
- Micro-depots: only worth it if replenishment returns are proven material.
- Broad referral rewards: useful tactically, but not a structural answer.

The one thing I’d still want to inspect is your route topology: a map of every completed stop plus actual start/end locations for four weeks. If the driving is mostly a few outlier tails, this is a book-design problem. If it is mostly customer-specific dates inside otherwise dense neighborhoods, the flexible-window product is the real lever.
