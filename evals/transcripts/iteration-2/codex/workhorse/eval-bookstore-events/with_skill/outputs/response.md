I wouldn’t drop author events entirely. I’d stop treating them as a standing free service and run a 90-day reset: only events that either pay for all staff time up front or deliberately test a higher-value role for the store.

The key reframing: an event that “breaks even” on book margin but consumes staff time is loss-making. Its only valid justification is that it buys something else measurable—new repeat customers, memberships, sponsor revenue, institutional relationships, or high-value signed inventory. The ABA’s benchmarking resources are useful for grounding the fully loaded labor calculation, rather than judging events from register sales alone. [ABA ABACUS resources](https://staging.bookweb.org/abacus-documents)

What I’d build instead:

- **A small, ticketed “reading society,” not a calendar full of appearances.** Four to eight genuinely special events a year; ticket includes the book, reserved seat, and perhaps a drink/partner perk. Sell a seasonal pass or membership to your best customers. The mechanism is advance, predictable revenue and a known audience—not hoping a crowd becomes profitable at the door. Biggest risk: regulars interpret this as paywalling culture; preserve a few free, lightweight community events.

- **A strict author/publisher event contract.** A date gets booked only with a preorder floor, a paid ticket threshold, or outside support (publisher co-op, venue partner, sponsor). Require the author’s audience-building commitments: email, social posts, store-linking, and an agreed event format. ABA itself encourages authors visiting stores to link an indie for sales fulfillment—make that normal operating practice, not an optional courtesy. [ABA IndieBound guidance](https://staging.bookweb.org/indiebound-0) Biggest risk: fewer events and pushback from publicists. That is likely a feature if the current pipeline is consuming scarce labor without return.

- **Move the labor-heavy events off your balance sheet.** For schools, libraries, universities, literary nonprofits, or local businesses, become the exclusive bookseller rather than the producer: they provide room, staffing, registration, and audience; you provide curation, books, and a clean order process. This converts events from retail theater into a distribution partnership. Biggest risk: reduced control and occasional lower per-event sales.

My most contrarian hypothesis: your most valuable “event customers” may happily prepay for belonging, but are not showing that demand because you currently offer unlimited free access. Test that before assuming events must be either free marketing or gone.

**This week’s experiment**

- **Hypothesis:** At least 25 existing customers will precommit to a $100–$150 seasonal reading-society pass that includes four book-included events.
- **Critical assumption tested:** Your core readers value guaranteed access and curation enough to pay in advance.
- **Experiment:** Email 100–150 of your most event-engaged customers with a real offer for a four-event fall series; cap it at 40 and take payment or a refundable deposit.
- **Resources:** 3–4 staff hours; no new event production.
- **Measured outcome:** Paid commitments/deposits in seven days.
- **Success threshold:** 25 commitments.
- **Failure threshold:** Fewer than 10.
- **Learned either way:** Whether your audience wants a durable event product, rather than individual free appearances.
- **If validated:** Build the series and use it to select authors.
- **If falsified:** Keep only partner-funded or preorder-guaranteed events.

Kill list:

- More social promotion for the same format — may fill seats, but does not fix the labor model.
- “Charge a small door fee” — often too little to change economics and can create friction.
- Add more events to find winners — worsens the bottleneck: staff attention.
- Livestream every event — adds production complexity without proving demand.
- Cancel everything permanently — throws away the community asset before testing a better economic container.

Assumptions: I’m assuming “barely break even” excludes fully loaded staff time, events aren’t crucial lease/sponsorship obligations, and you have an email list of prior attendees. If staff time is already included and events still only break even, the case for a sharp cut is stronger: retain only strategic, partner-funded dates.
