I wouldn’t drop them entirely—yet. I’d stop treating every author event as a retail transaction that must break even that night.

Instead, run a 90-day reset:

- Hold fewer events: only authors with a real local draw, strong preorder potential, or a clear strategic reason.
- Require preorders/tickets for most events; make the ticket redeemable for the book.
- Cap staff time with a repeatable event playbook and a single event lead.
- Use off-site partners—libraries, schools, cafés, venues—for larger or labor-heavy programs.
- Track full cost: staff hours, setup/cleanup, fees, discounts, inventory risk, plus event-night and 30-day sales, new customer emails, repeat visits, and partner value.

Then classify them:

| Keep | Redesign | Stop |
|---|---|---|
| reliably sell books or build valuable customer relationships | good attendance but poor conversion; change to ticket/preorder format | low attendance, low conversion, high labor, no strategic benefit |

My threshold: an event should either make a modest direct contribution after labor, *or* clearly function as customer acquisition/community marketing with a defined budget. “Barely break even and exhausts staff” is not a sustainable middle category.

The American Booksellers Association specifically frames event ROI as something to track against staff time, and its financial benchmarking resources can help put the costs in context. [ABA event-ROI session](https://staging.bookweb.org/education?field_education_2ndtags_tid%5B0%5D=731&page=1) · [ABACUS benchmarking](https://staging.bookweb.org/abacus-documents)

If the reset doesn’t reveal a small, high-performing event program, then retire author events without guilt. Your bookstore can still be a literary community hub through book clubs, school partnerships, signed-stock drops, and occasional marquee events—without becoming an unpaid event-production company.
