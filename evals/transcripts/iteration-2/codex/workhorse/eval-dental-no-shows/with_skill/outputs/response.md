The strongest fix is to stop treating a six-month-ahead hygiene booking as a firm appointment. Treat it as a **care commitment that must become an actively claimed slot** closer to the visit—then use released capacity to fill openings.

A 48-hour SMS arrives after many patients have already silently decided not to come. It can remind, but it does not create commitment or recover the chair time.

Assumption: these are mostly routine recall visits booked far in advance. If they are largely acute or treatment-linked visits, use a different design.

1. **Replace fixed long-range appointments with “care-due windows.”**  
   Rather than booking every patient a precise time six months ahead, invite them 2–4 weeks before they are due to choose from several slots. Patients who do select a slot are more likely to have made a real commitment.

   - Mechanism: shortens the gap between intention and appointment.
   - Risk: the schedule may look less full far in advance. Measure completed hygiene care within 30 days of due date—not just no-show rate.

2. **Make confirmation an activation event, not a reminder.**  
   About 7 days out, ask patients to tap one of three choices: “I’m coming,” “I need another time,” or “release this time.” Unactivated appointments get a brief personal outreach, then are released to a standby list 48–72 hours out.

   - Mechanism: patients either recommit or reveal their likely absence while there is time to replace them.
   - The key metric is “unactivated slots rescued,” not replies.

3. **Create a same-week hygiene standby list.**  
   At checkout and in recall messages, invite patients to opt in for short-notice openings. When a slot is released, text a small, relevant group: “A hygiene visit opened Tuesday at 2:00—reply YES to claim it.” Keep it generic and privacy-safe.

   - Mechanism: even if no-shows persist, empty-chair loss falls.
   - This is often more valuable than trying to eliminate every missed appointment.

Most contrarian hypothesis: **the practice should deliberately stop holding its scarcest prime-time hygiene slots for patients who have not actively claimed them.** For patients with repeated missed visits, offer same-week or off-peak booking by default, while allowing exceptions for genuine transportation, work, disability, or access needs. This needs careful, consistent policy so it does not become punitive or inequitable.

Cheapest high-information test:

- **Hypothesis:** active claiming plus release-to-standby cuts no-shows from 14% to 8% or lower without reducing hygiene completion within 30 days.
- **Critical assumption:** non-confirmers are materially more likely to miss, and released slots can be refilled.
- **Experiment:** randomly assign 150 upcoming hygiene appointments to the current 48-hour SMS process and 150 to the activation process for six weeks.
- **Treatment:** 7-day three-choice message; personal follow-up for no response; release unclaimed slots 72 hours out; send openings to the standby list.
- **Measure:** no-show rate, late-cancel rate, released slots refilled, and completed hygiene visits within 30 days.
- **Success threshold:** treatment no-shows ≤8%, with 30-day completion no worse than control by more than 5 percentage points.
- **Failure threshold:** no meaningful reduction after 150 appointments, or completion falls materially.
- **If validated:** make activation standard, then use patient-level history to tune timing and slot access.

Avoid spending much energy on:

- More reminder messages: likely incremental at best.
- Generic no-show fees: common, can damage trust and access.
- AI-written reminder copy: an efficiency tweak, not a commitment mechanism.
- Blanket overbooking: risks bad patient experience and staff overload.
- Rewards/gamification: usually too weak relative to real scheduling friction.

Before rollout, segment the 14% by lead time, day/time, prior attendance, insurance/payment status, and whether the patient ever replied. That will reveal whether this is mainly a commitment problem, a transportation/work-hours problem, or a specific scheduling-pattern problem.
