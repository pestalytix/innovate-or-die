Treat this as an activation and value-realization problem, not merely an onboarding-content problem. A 45-minute call plus documentation may explain the product, but it may not get a subcontractor to a durable operational outcome before the first billing/renewal decision.

Focus the first 90 days on one measurable “job to be done,” such as submitting bids faster, collecting required documentation, reducing change-order leakage, or improving field-to-office coordination.

- Replace the single guided call with a 30/60/90-day success program. Define a customer-specific outcome on day one, agree on milestones, and have a named owner on both sides. Trigger structured check-ins based on usage and milestone completion—not just time elapsed.

- Build role-specific, workflow-native activation. Estimators, project managers, foremen, and finance staff should each get a short path to their first useful action. Avoid generic product tours; guide users through importing a live project, inviting a GC/customer, sending a real document, or completing a real workflow.

- Make implementation a service, not an optional customer task. Offer concierge data import, template setup, integrations, and first-project configuration. For smaller accounts, standardize this into a fixed-fee or bundled “launch package.”

- Instrument leading indicators of retention. Identify the behaviors that correlate with surviving past 90 days—for example: a project created, three teammates active, an integration connected, recurring weekly use, or a workflow completed. Score accounts against those signals and automatically route low-health customers to human outreach or in-product interventions.

- Create an early-warning system architecture. Event-stream product usage into a customer-health model; combine usage with support tickets, failed integrations, billing signals, and stakeholder engagement. Use it to trigger tailored playbooks, rather than relying on CSM intuition or periodic account reviews.

- Reduce time-to-value through integrations and defaults. Construction teams will resist duplicate entry. Integrate with the systems where work already happens—accounting, bid management, document storage, email, and possibly SMS. Provide opinionated templates and sensible defaults so a usable workspace exists immediately.

- Redesign pricing to align with realized value. If customers pay full recurring fees before a project is live, they may churn before value appears. Consider an implementation-inclusive annual plan, a ramped first-quarter price, per-project pricing, usage/value-based pricing, or a paid pilot that converts only after agreed success criteria are met. Be careful: discounts alone can hide, rather than solve, weak activation.

- Segment the business model. High-touch implementation may be economically justified for larger subcontractors, while smaller firms may need a narrower, self-serve product with a faster setup path. A single onboarding model is rarely right for both.

- Add a managed-service layer. If the product depends on ongoing administrative work—compliance tracking, document chasing, system configuration—offer it as a paid or bundled managed service. Some early “churn” is really customers declining to become process administrators.

- Reconsider the product boundary. If adoption requires all project stakeholders to change behavior, offer a lightweight external experience for GCs, suppliers, or field teams: email-to-workflow, mobile forms, link-based approvals, and no-login participation. Network friction is a product-architecture issue, not an onboarding issue.

- Strengthen champion resilience. Early accounts often churn when the original buyer is busy, leaves, or fails to bring the field team along. Require multi-threaded adoption: at least one executive sponsor, one operational owner, and several active users. Build shared dashboards and automated value reports that help the champion defend the purchase internally.

- Use cancellation and cohort learning rigorously. Segment churn by trade, company size, acquisition channel, integration status, number of active users, and first-value milestone. Interview churned customers within days, then run controlled experiments against the biggest failure mode rather than broadly “improving onboarding.”

A practical near-term sequence: first determine the two or three activation events most predictive of 90-day retention; then create a white-glove path that reliably gets new accounts to those events; finally automate the successful pieces through templates, integrations, lifecycle messaging, and health-triggered intervention.
