The strongest thesis: early churn is probably less an onboarding problem than a failure to reach a job-specific, recurring proof of value before the customer’s attention moves elsewhere. A 45-minute call and documentation transfer knowledge; they do not install a workflow that survives a busy construction week. At 6% monthly churn, even if it stayed flat, roughly 17% of new logos disappear by month three.

Most important reframing: stop treating “customer activated” as “attended onboarding.” Define it as: a named live project produces a useful operational artifact repeatedly, with a responsible internal owner and the required data flowing in. The likely inherited constraint is that all subscribers deserve the same self-service-plus-call launch. Construction work is project- and timing-driven; many accounts may simply be bought at the wrong moment.

Assumptions: this product supports an operational workflow (rather than purely occasional reporting), and customers can identify a live job during sale or implementation. If either is false, prioritize a different value metric.

## Top opportunities

1. **Sell a production launch, not access to software**

   Make the first commercial unit a scoped “first live-job deployment”: one workflow, one project, one accountable customer owner, and a written definition of the recurring output to be live within 30 days. Subscription billing begins—or a meaningful portion converts from implementation credit—only once that outcome is delivered.

   Mechanism: the vendor absorbs some activation risk, while the customer must supply a live job, data access, and an owner. Those commitments force the conditions that cause retention instead of merely teaching features.

   Why it is non-obvious: it changes revenue timing and implementation economics, rather than adding another onboarding email sequence.

   Biggest failure mode: the company accidentally becomes an unbounded services business. Prevent that with a standardized deployment menu, explicit customer prerequisites, and a narrow “one workflow” scope.

   Radical version: price by active project or verified operational outcome, with a platform minimum, rather than by seats/users.

2. **Make the product useful through the tools people already use**

   Architect the first workflow around construction’s existing communications surface—email, mobile links, spreadsheets, and the systems used by GCs/customers—rather than requiring every person to log into a new portal. Examples: automatically generated change-order status, compliance packet, daily field capture, billing-ready evidence, or look-ahead artifact that is sent to the people already involved in the job.

   Mechanism: a recurring artifact tied to a project creates a habit loop and exposes value to multiple stakeholders. The product becomes part of work completion, not an admin task someone remembers to do.

   Why it is disproportionate: it reduces both training burden and the “single champion leaves / gets busy” churn risk. It also creates external switching friction when a GC, customer, or field crew relies on the output.

   Biggest failure mode: brittle integrations or solving a workflow customers do not value enough to change for. Start with import/export and structured inbound email before building deep integrations.

3. **Change who implements—and who gets paid for retention**

   Build a certified deployment channel for the people already trusted to set up subcontractor systems: construction software consultants, bookkeepers/CFO firms, trade associations, and selected reseller partners. Pay part of their compensation at day 90 only if the account meets the live-job activation metric and remains active.

   Mechanism: the implementation resource has contextual knowledge, better access to the owner/data, and a direct financial reason to prevent “we meant to use it” abandonment.

   Why it is non-obvious: this is an operating and incentive redesign, not a customer-success staffing expansion.

   Biggest failure mode: partners sell indiscriminately or lack product discipline. Begin with 3–5 partners, prescribed deployment playbooks, and retention-based—not merely signup-based—compensation.

## Most contrarian hypothesis

Do not try to retain every new logo. Refuse or defer activation for accounts without a named live job, an operational owner, and a source of required data. Offer a “ready-to-launch” holding program instead.

This may lower near-term bookings while improving genuine retention, implementation cost, referenceability, and sales efficiency. Track this honestly: report both qualified-cohort churn and all-lead conversion, so improved churn cannot be faked by filtering.

## Cheapest high-information experiment

Hypothesis: requiring a named live job and selling a bounded production launch materially improves 90-day retention versus the current guided-call cohort.

Critical assumption tested: prospects will accept a launch commitment and can provide the inputs needed to produce a recurring, job-native output.

Experiment: for the next 20 qualified opportunities, replace the standard onboarding offer with: “We will deploy one live workflow in 30 days; you nominate the project, owner, and data source. The subscription starts after the agreed artifact is live.” Keep a matched 20-account control cohort on the current process where practical.

Resources: one product/CS owner; a fixed 30-minute readiness assessment per prospect; no new product build beyond manual import/export and current capabilities.

Measured outcome: acceptance rate; percent reaching recurring live output within 30 days; weekly use of that output through day 60; cancels by day 90.

Success threshold: at least 12 of 20 accept the commitment, at least 70% reach live output in 30 days, and the activated cohort’s projected 90-day churn is at least 30% lower than control.

Failure threshold: fewer than 8 accept, or fewer than half can supply a live job/data/owner despite sales qualification.

Learned either way: whether the bottleneck is commercial timing/readiness versus the product’s ability to deliver a valued workflow.

If validated: standardize the deployment offer and build the smallest reusable job-launch templates.

If falsified: investigate workflow value and buyer fit before investing in onboarding automation.

## Conventional foundation worth doing in parallel

Instrument the first 90 days around causal milestones, not logins: named job, data connected, first artifact delivered, first repeat use, second stakeholder engaged, and executive value review. Interview churned accounts by failure point. This is conventional, but it is the measurement layer needed to tell whether the bolder changes work.

## Compact kill list

- More documentation: improves knowledge availability, but does not create a live work loop.
- More guided-call time: often increases service cost without changing customer commitment or timing.
- Generic “AI onboarding assistant”: efficiency enhancement, not a structural retention mechanism.
- Gamified feature adoption: optimizes product activity, which may be unrelated to job outcomes.
- Blanket discounts/longer free trials: delays the decision without resolving absence of a live use case.

What may still be missing: the churn may be dominated by a specific segment—trade, company size, buyer role, season, or integration dependency. Before broad rollout, cut the cohort by those dimensions; one concentrated failure pattern can justify deliberately narrowing the ICP rather than redesigning onboarding for everyone.
