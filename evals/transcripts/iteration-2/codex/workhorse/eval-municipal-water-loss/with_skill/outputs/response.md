The strongest no-capex move is to stop treating “22% NRW” as one problem. First separate it into real leakage, apparent loss (metering/billing), and authorized-but-unbilled use; the right operational action differs for each.

Assumption: “no capital” permits staff time, existing telemetry/valves/meters, and modest operating spend such as meter testing or overtime—not asset purchases.

| Approach | Mechanism | Evidence that confirms it | Evidence that refutes it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Build a defensible water balance from existing records | Finds whether the 22% estimate is real loss, bad production measurement, billing/meter error, or unrecorded authorized use. | Reconciliation of treatment output, interties, tank changes, billed consumption, hydrant/flushing, and known operational use closes materially better; production-meter or billing anomalies are found. | Independently checked meters and reconciled records still show a persistent, well-localized physical deficit. |
| Correct apparent losses | Fix read-to-bill exceptions, inactive accounts with consumption, meter-reading/data-transfer errors, incorrect multipliers, estimated bills, and unauthorized connections/use. This improves billed volume and revenue, though not necessarily water physically saved. | Targeted account review finds systematic under-registration, billing gaps, estimated-read bias, or illegal/unbilled consumption; billed use rises without comparable production increase. | Random sample tests show customer and source meters are within expected accuracy and account/billing exceptions are immaterial. |
| Reduce excess pressure using existing PRVs and pump controls | Leakage flow and break frequency generally rise with pressure. A controlled reduction during low-demand hours can reduce real loss without new hardware. | In a zone with an existing controllable PRV/pump setting, lower pressure produces a sustained reduction in minimum-night inflow after adjusting for legitimate night demand, while service and fire-flow requirements remain met. | Minimum-night flow does not fall materially, or required customer pressure/fire protection cannot be maintained. |
| Shift leak work from “repair when found” to “minimize leak-days” | The avoidable volume is leak flow × time to isolate/repair. Faster triage, after-hours repair for high-flow leaks, and verification after repair can save water with the same crews. | Work-order history shows long detection-to-isolation/repair times or a small number of high-flow leaks causing most estimated lost volume; zone inflow falls after repairs. | Repair intervals are already short and post-repair flow data show only trivial reductions. |
| Use existing data to target field search | Prioritize areas showing unexplained night flow, recurring breaks, pressure excursions, or treatment-to-zone imbalance; use existing crews, valves, and any already-owned acoustic tools. | A small priority list finds disproportionate leaks or unauthorized use relative to random patrols; confirmed repairs reduce the local night-flow baseline. | Targeted areas produce no more findings or flow reduction than routine work. |
| Control and measure utility-owned unbilled consumption | Log hydrant use, construction water, flushing, main breaks, tank overflows, plant washwater, and other operational withdrawals; defer or optimize discretionary water-intensive work within water-quality rules. | Logged quantities explain a meaningful share of the residual; changes lower system input without violating water-quality obligations. | The volume is small, already well measured, or reductions compromise regulatory water-quality/operational requirements. |
| Establish a crew feedback loop | Give operations and repair crews a weekly “before/after flow” scorecard, not an abstract NRW target. It makes leak verification and repair quality observable. | Repair teams can repeatedly associate completed jobs with sustained reductions in zone/system flow and use the result to improve prioritization. | Data cannot be tied to work locations/times, or effects are drowned out by demand variation. |

The most important reframing: committed capital blocks pipe replacement, but it does not block reducing the volume of water that is mismeasured, unbilled, over-pressurized, or left leaking for too long. Some measures lower physical loss; others only make the 22% estimate and revenue position more accurate. Keep those outcomes separate.

The three highest-value hypotheses to test are:

1. **The 22% is materially overstated by apparent loss or unrecorded authorized use.**  
   Confirm with an independently reconciled monthly water balance and targeted meter/billing checks. Refute if checks validate the figures and the residual remains physical.

2. **A few pressure-sensitive zones account for a disproportionate share of real loss.**  
   Confirm with a controlled pressure-versus-minimum-night-flow test using existing controls. Refute if flow is insensitive or service constraints prevent pressure reduction.

3. **The utility’s largest controllable loss is repair duration, not leak discovery.**  
   Confirm by ranking historical leaks by estimated flow × repair delay and verifying post-repair flow reductions. Refute if delays are already low or delayed leaks are low-flow.

A cheap, high-information experiment:

- **Hypothesis:** Existing data can identify actions that reduce either real loss or apparent loss within 30 days.
- **Critical assumption tested:** The utility has sufficiently trustworthy treatment output, billing, work-order, and pressure/flow records.
- **Experiment:** Run a four-week “NRW truth sprint”: reconcile one month of water balance; select one pressure-controllable zone and one high-leak-work-order area; audit the 50 largest billing/read exceptions.
- **Resources:** A utility lead, billing analyst, operations supervisor, and GIS/work-order analyst; existing data and controls.
- **Measured outcome:** Reconciled residual volume; validated billing recovery; change in normalized minimum-night flow; estimated leak-days eliminated.
- **Success threshold:** Identify and validate actions totaling at least a meaningful, repeatable share of the current residual—enough to justify making them standard operating practice.
- **Failure threshold:** Records cannot be reconciled, the pressure trial yields no sustained flow change, and targeted account/work-order reviews reveal only immaterial losses.
- **If validated:** Institutionalize a monthly water balance, pressure operating envelopes, and leak-days prioritization.
- **If falsified:** Treat the 22% estimate as uncertain; focus on measurement integrity and use the next capital cycle for better district metering and targeted renewal.

A useful contrarian hypothesis is that the first “saved water” may come from proving the estimate wrong and recovering revenue, rather than finding a dramatic hidden pipe leak. That is not a disappointment—it prevents three years of capital decisions from being aimed at the wrong loss mechanism.
