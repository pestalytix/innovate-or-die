The missing lever is probably not better routing—it’s **changing what you promise customers so density can exist at all**.

With 900 quarterly accounts, each customer’s exact service date is usually an inherited constraint, not a real one. If techs drive 40% of the day after route optimization, the schedule is likely scattering commitments across the towns faster than software can repair them.

Assumptions: exterior quarterly service can usually be performed without the homeowner present; your three towns have meaningful pockets of account density; and you can offer a different service window without violating local pesticide-notice rules. Verify the last point locally.

## Reframe

You do not really have a “windshield time” problem. You have a **service-density market design** problem:

- You sell a quarterly treatment.
- Operations then tries to fulfill many individualized dates.
- The customer captures date convenience; you absorb the travel cost.

Instead, sell a reliable quarterly outcome within a known **zone service week**. Route sequence becomes almost trivial once calendar commitments stop fighting geography.

## Three high-leverage moves

1. **Convert to zoned service weeks**

   Divide each town into compact zones. Assign every account a recurring week per quarter, with a published annual calendar; within that week, service is done without a promised day/time.

   Offer two tiers:
   - “Route-smart service”: scheduled in its zone’s service week.
   - “Date-choice service”: a real premium for customers who require a fixed day/window.

   Mechanism: you stop spending field capacity to satisfy free scheduling preference. A tech should work one tight zone per day, not touch all three towns.

   Biggest failure mode: allowing endless exceptions recreates the old system. Give one free reschedule per year; anything beyond that moves the account to the premium tier or next zone cycle.

2. **Make density a product, not an accident**

   For sparse edges, stop pricing every account identically. Use a “community service day” / “neighborhood rate”:

   - Existing accounts in a sparse pocket get a credit when a nearby referral joins the same zone.
   - New customers choose either the lower zone-day price or a higher flexible-date price.
   - Stop taking low-margin “orphan” accounts unless they pay for the travel they create.

   Mechanism: acquisition fills gaps in routes instead of merely adding revenue and more driving. Your sales map should identify holes between existing stops—not just leads.

   Biggest failure mode: discounting customers you would have won anyway. Only deploy it in explicitly defined low-density cells.

3. **Treat territory shape as a capital allocation decision**

   Plot the last two quarters of completed stops and identify the outer 10–20% that consume a disproportionate share of miles. Then consider an uncomfortable option: raise their price, move them to a limited service day, refer them out, or explore an account swap with another reputable operator whose accounts are concentrated where yours are.

   Mechanism: the route boundary—not the route order—is often the actual cost center.

   Biggest failure mode: overreacting before proving which stops create deadhead mileage. Measure first.

## Most contrarian hypothesis

**Your best growth may be saying no to geographically wrong revenue.** An account that adds $X in revenue but forces a daily cross-town transition can be worse than no account; it consumes scarce capacity while making every nearby account slower to serve.

## Cheapest high-information experiment

**Hypothesis:** Customers will accept a recurring zone service week, and it will cut drive time enough to outweigh exception handling.

**Critical assumption tested:** At least 80% of customers will accept a service week rather than a specific day.

**Experiment:** Pick one dense 50–75 account area. For its next quarter, notify customers: “Your service is guaranteed during the week of [date]; no appointment needed for exterior service. Choose a different week if necessary.” Keep a small exception list, but do not promise dates.

**Resources:** One office script, a map, your current scheduling system, and 2–3 hours of admin time.

**Measured outcome:** Acceptance rate; drive minutes per completed service; exceptions; callbacks/cancellations.

**Success threshold:** ≥80% accept with no manual exception, and drive minutes per stop fall ≥20%.

**Failure threshold:** <65% accept, or exceptions eliminate most of the drive-time gain.

**Learned either way:** Whether customer-date flexibility is your binding constraint, versus territory geometry or depot/stocking practices.

**If validated:** Roll out by zone at renewal, grandfathering only genuinely constrained accounts into a paid fixed-date tier.

**If falsified:** Audit first/last-leg miles and test decentralized starts or a small secure stocking point—while checking pesticide-storage and vehicle requirements.

## Kill list

- “Buy better optimization software” — you already tested the obvious; software cannot create density against hard appointment constraints.
- “Make techs faster” — may improve productivity, but it does not remove road hours.
- “Add another truck” — increases capacity cost while preserving the travel architecture.
- “Discount broadly for referrals” — generic growth can worsen route sprawl.
- “Promise tighter arrival windows” — directly intensifies the cause of the problem.

What I’d inspect next is a map colored by **drive minutes per completed stop**, plus the first and last stop of every route. Those two views will tell you whether the principal leak is scattered service commitments, sparse boundary accounts, or daily depot deadhead.
