You’re likely past the point where better sequencing alone can solve it. If technicians spend ~40% of the day driving, the underlying problem is usually the *service model and territory design*, not just route optimization.

The biggest levers to investigate:

- **Geographic density per service day.** Quarterly customers should be assigned to fixed service “zones” and recurring weeks—not scheduled individually across all three towns. A tech’s Tuesday should be one compact micro-territory, even if that means a customer’s exact service date shifts slightly quarter to quarter.

- **Route ownership.** Give each technician a stable territory and book all callbacks, re-services, and add-ons into that same territory. Floating work and “whoever is free” dispatch creates invisible drive time.

- **Customer promise design.** If you promise narrow appointment windows, accommodate many customer-requested dates, or react quickly to low-value reschedules, you are buying windshield time. Consider an “arrival day” model with an optional paid appointment-window upgrade.

- **Callbacks and one-offs.** Measure their travel separately. A modest callback rate can consume a disproportionate share of driving because those trips break route density. Bundle non-urgent callbacks into dedicated zone days; use remote triage before dispatching.

- **Service-time economics.** Identify accounts that are geographically isolated, routinely inaccessible, or have unusually high callback/service demand. Raise price, alter service terms, consolidate timing, or—in a few cases—stop serving them. Every outlier account can damage the day for 20 nearby accounts.

- **Start/end locations and restocking.** Techs commuting from home, returning to the shop mid-day, or needing supplies can turn an efficient route into an inefficient shift. Standardized truck stock, overnight replenishment, and appropriately placed vehicle staging can matter more than another routing algorithm.

- **Sales geography.** Stop treating every new sale as equally good. Put a density rule into sales compensation: reward accounts that fill existing route clusters and price distant accounts for their true operational cost. Marketing should target holes within existing zones, not merely the three-town footprint.

- **Route capacity targets.** Set an operational target such as “X stops, Y production hours, and no more than Z drive minutes per zone-day.” Without this, teams optimize individual routes but never redesign the system around profitable density.

I’d start with a four-week analysis of every tech-day: drive minutes, service minutes, number of stops, territory, callbacks, customer-requested times, and start/end location. Map the results by micro-zone. You’ll probably find a small number of route days, exception types, and sparse-edge customers creating most of the problem.

The goal is not “shorter routes.” It’s **dense, repeatable route days with exceptions priced or constrained**.
