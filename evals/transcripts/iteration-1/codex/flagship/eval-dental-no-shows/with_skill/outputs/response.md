The practice should stop treating this as a reminder problem. It is primarily an **early-information and perishable-capacity problem**: identify uncertain appointments sooner, then refill them before the chair time expires.

Set a 90-day internal target of reducing unproductive hygiene appointments from 14% to below 9%, counting a cancellation that gets refilled as a success.

## The key reframing

The real requirement is that the patient, hygienist, room, and equipment coincide. The inherited assumption is that one patient must own an exact appointment—often booked six months earlier—until they arrive or fail.

Measure:

- Completed hygiene hours
- Openings released at least 72 hours ahead
- Percentage of released time refilled
- Staff minutes per rescued appointment
- Patient complaints and attrition

Dental research repeatedly identifies prior no-shows and booking lead time as useful predictors, although each practice should validate its own patterns. Evidence also suggests multiple reminders can outperform one, and voice contact sometimes outperforms SMS. ([Dental prediction study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9680883/), [notification systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5093388/))

## Top opportunities

1. **Risk-triggered commitment and recovery**

Seven days ahead, send higher-risk patients three choices: “Keeping,” “Need to change,” or “Not sure.” Personally call only nonresponders and uncertain patients. Keep the existing 48-hour SMS.

The mechanism is earlier detection—not simply more reminders. When someone releases a slot, immediately cascade it to a recently refreshed “available this week” list.

Test: compare this workflow with the existing process across comparable appointments. Abandon it if completed-or-refilled visits improve by less than two percentage points or the recovered contribution does not justify staff time.

2. **Buy timely truth instead of punishing failure**

After confirming applicable state and payer rules, test a small account credit or valuable booking privilege for releasing a slot at least 72 hours early. The practice is purchasing recoverable information rather than collecting a penalty after the valuable time has disappeared.

Test it on alternating high-risk appointments. Stop if timely releases do not increase by at least 10 percentage points, fewer than half the released slots refill, or recovered contribution is less than twice the program cost.

A late-cancellation fee remains an option—the ADA discusses 24- or 48-hour policies—but it should not be the centerpiece and must be checked against local requirements and payer contracts. ([ADA guidance](https://www.ada.org/resources/practice/practice-management/cancellations))

3. **Stop making fragile six-month promises**

For selected patients, replace checkout booking with a due-month reminder and appointments offered two to four weeks ahead. Patients requiring advance certainty can retain conventional booking.

Test this with patients due within the next 8–12 weeks. Stop if attendance fails to improve by five percentage points or fewer than 80% successfully book when contacted near their due date.

The biggest risk is replacing no-shows with never-booked openings, so monitor both attendance and total hygiene utilization.

## Most contrarian hypothesis

Repeat no-show patients may attend more reliably if they **lose distant-booking privileges but gain generous same-week access**.

Their problem may not be unwillingness; their lives may simply be incompatible with long-range promises. Pilot this voluntarily and provide a route back to advance booking. Review it for equity impacts before expansion.

## Cheapest high-information experiment

**Hypothesis:** For high-risk hygiene appointments, a seven-day uncertainty check plus rapid refill process will increase completed-or-refilled appointments from 86% to at least 91%.

**Critical assumption tested:** Endangered appointments can be identified and released early enough to replace them.

**Experiment:** Review the previous 90 days and flag appointments using only prior missed visits and booking lead time. Alternate the next 150 eligible appointments between:

- Control: existing 48-hour SMS
- Test: seven-day “Keeping / Change / Not sure” message, one follow-up call, existing SMS, and immediate standby outreach for released slots

Do not introduce deposits or automatic cancellation during this test.

**Resources:** Approximately four setup hours, 10–15 staff minutes daily, existing scheduling and messaging tools.

**Measured outcome:** Completed-or-refilled rate, no-show rate, releases at least 72 hours ahead, refill rate, staff time, and complaints.

**Success threshold:** At least a five-point improvement in completed-or-refilled visits and at least 60% of early openings refilled.

**Failure threshold:** Less than a two-point improvement, refill below 30%, or staff cost exceeding recovered contribution.

**Learned either way:** Whether the bottleneck is early warning, standby demand, or a different cause entirely.

**If validated:** Automate the seven-day branch, then separately test which component—message, call, or standby pool—creates the lift.

**If falsified:** Interview the last 15–20 no-show patients and segment the causes: transport, anxiety, cost uncertainty, work, childcare, scheduling error, or poor prior experience.

## Kill list

- Blanket additional reminders: potentially helpful, but wastes effort on reliable patients.
- A prediction dashboard alone: identifies risk without changing the outcome.
- Immediate broad double-booking: risks congestion, rushed care, and staff burnout.
- Blanket deposits or fees: may create access barriers while failing to refill openings.
- Firing repeat offenders: protects the schedule by sacrificing care access and lifetime patient value.

## What may still be missing

The practice needs to determine whether the 14% is concentrated by patient history, lead time, weekday, appointment type, booking source, insurance, or particular households. It should also examine practice-created failures—incorrect contact information, office rescheduling, unclear costs, long waits, or negative previous visits.

Any deposits, credits, automatic releases, or differentiated booking rules should be reviewed for state, payer, disability-access, and equity implications before broad rollout.
