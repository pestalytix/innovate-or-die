A 14% hygiene no-show rate is high enough that the practice needs a confirmation-and-recovery system, not merely another reminder. The current SMS tells patients about the appointment but apparently does not require a decision or trigger staff action.

### Recommended workflow

1. **Confirm when booking**
   - Verify the mobile number and preferred contact method.
   - Send the date, time, provider, location, approximate duration, and an “add to calendar” link.
   - Ask patients to notify the practice promptly if work, transportation, childcare, anxiety, or cost may interfere.

2. **Replace the passive 48-hour SMS**
   Use an actionable message:

   > “Hi [First name], please reply **YES** to confirm your hygiene visit on [day] at [time], or **R** to reschedule. If we don’t hear from you by [deadline], we’ll call because another patient may be waiting for this time.”

   A cancellation or reschedule is operationally much better than an empty chair. Research consistently finds reminders effective, while multiple notifications outperform a single one; reminders also work partly by prompting earlier cancellation and rebooking. [BMJ Open systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5093388/), [TURNUP evidence synthesis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25642537/)

3. **Create an exception queue**
   - At 24 hours, staff call every patient who has not replied.
   - Unreachable, previously unreliable patients receive focused attention—not a blanket call to everyone.
   - Send a brief same-day reminder to confirmed patients, especially for early-morning appointments.
   - Do not treat an automated delivery receipt as confirmation.

4. **Build a hygiene “quick-fill” list**
   Maintain patients who want earlier appointments, grouped by preferred day and time. When someone cancels, send the opening to a small group and give it to the first respondent. The goal is to measure both the no-show rate and the percentage of vacated hours successfully refilled.

5. **Address repeat behavior individually**
   After one missed appointment, contact the patient without shaming them and record the actual barrier. After repeated misses, consider:
   - Shorter booking horizons
   - Same-week or same-day scheduling
   - A card hold or modest reservation deposit
   - A consistently enforced missed-appointment policy

   Any fee or deposit policy should be disclosed before booking, comply with payer contracts and local rules, and allow reasonable hardship or emergency exceptions. Avoid broad overbooking initially—it can create excessive waits when attendance improves.

### Diagnose before blaming patients

For the previous 3–6 months, segment broken appointments by:

- No-show versus late cancellation
- New versus established patient
- Days between booking and appointment
- Day, time, hygienist, and appointment duration
- Prior missed appointments
- Insurance/self-pay and expected patient cost
- Whether the SMS was delivered and whether the patient replied
- Stated reason: forgot, work, transportation, childcare, illness, anxiety, or unexpected cost

This will reveal whether the dominant problem is forgetting, appointments booked too far ahead, financial uncertainty, poor contact data, or friction in cancelling.

### 60-day pilot

Run the new workflow on alternating days or randomly assigned appointments:

- **Control:** existing 48-hour passive SMS
- **Pilot:** booking confirmation + actionable 48-hour SMS + 24-hour call for nonresponders + quick-fill list

Track no-shows, late cancellations, filled hygiene hours, staff minutes, and patient complaints. A sensible initial target is reducing no-shows from 14% to below 10%, then toward 7–8%. For every 100 hygiene appointments, reaching 8% recovers roughly six appointments—before counting openings saved through earlier cancellation and refilling.

The essential change is: **make every reminder request an action, immediately work the nonresponse list, and have a ready mechanism to refill released time.**
