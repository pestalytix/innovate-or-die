I wouldn’t drop events entirely. I’d cut the calendar sharply and make the remaining events earn their place.

For the next 90 days, I’d:

- Keep only events with a clear audience: established draw, strong local following, active partner organization, or unusually good fit with your customers.
- Use advance tickets—ideally bundled with the book or redeemable as store credit. Bundled and ticketed models are already common among independents, while stores such as BookPeople limit requests and tie signing access to purchasing from the store. [BookPeople](https://bookpeople.com/event-requests-faq), [Café con Libros](https://www.cafeconlibrosbk.com/event-partnership-agreement)
- Set a presale threshold. If an event hasn’t sold enough tickets 7–10 days beforehand, convert it to a short signing, reschedule it, or cancel it.
- Require authors, publishers, or community partners to provide concrete promotion—not merely “we’ll post on social.”
- Favor repeatable formats—genre nights, ticketed workshops, author panels, supper-club partnerships—over labor-intensive one-off readings.
- Move events off-site when a library, brewery, school, or arts organization can provide the room, audience, or staffing.
- Give unsolicited/local-author events a standardized low-labor format: shared showcase, consignment signing table, or paid venue package.

Calculate each event this way:

`event contribution = tickets + book gross profit + other gross profit − staff hours − fees − refreshments − marketing − lost selling capacity`

Don’t judge it by revenue or books sold. Count setup, emails, receiving/returns, furniture moving, cleanup, and management time. Also record new email subscribers and 30-day repeat purchases, but don’t give “community value” unlimited power to excuse losses.

My rule would be: an event continues only if it covers its fully loaded cost and produces a reasonable contribution, or if it achieves a specific strategic goal with a predetermined annual subsidy. For example: “We will spend up to $3,000 annually on six mission-driven events.” That turns community programming into a conscious investment instead of a recurring mystery loss.

Some events will reveal themselves as marketing rather than commerce. That’s fine—but then compare them with what the same staff time and money could produce through book clubs, school sales, newsletters, or customer outreach.

My likely endpoint would be 50–75% fewer author events, almost all ticketed, bundled, partnered, or demonstrably profitable. If even that smaller calendar fails the 90-day test, I’d drop conventional readings and retain only signings, workshops, and occasional flagship events.
