I’d stop the current model—but not permanently abolish every event yet.

Freeze new bookings for 60 days, honor existing commitments, then reopen with perhaps 20–30% of the former calendar. Staff capacity should be treated like cash: nobody gets to spend it without naming the return.

This looks structural, not like a marketing failure. Research involving bookstore and library organizers found cost, labor, and space were the principal barriers to literary events. Some stores now explicitly charge authors or publishers because of that workload. [Australia Reads/University of Melbourne](https://australiareads.org.au/research/live-literature-in-australia/), [Indy Reads’ current event model](https://indyreads.org/get-involved/submit-an-event/author-event/).

### The important reframe

Don’t ask, “Are events profitable?”

Ask:

> Which benefits actually require a live event produced by my employees—and who should pay for those benefits?

Discovery, signed books, community, author contact, publisher relationships, and institutional sales are separable. Most don’t require your staff to chase publicity materials, arrange chairs, moderate, troubleshoot A/V, and clean up after hours.

Before calling an event break-even, include preparation, promotion, inventory handling, setup, overtime, cleanup, payment fees—and the value of work displaced.

### The three-door calendar

Every future event must qualify through one door:

1. **Commercial:** A hosting fee, minimum guarantee, bulk order, or credible preorders cover fully loaded costs and produce at least 1.5× your normal contribution per staff-hour.

2. **Strategic:** It serves a specifically named publisher, institution, or customer relationship. Cap these—perhaps four annually—so “relationship value” cannot become an unlimited loophole.

3. **Mission:** You consciously subsidize it from a fixed annual cultural-programming budget. Patron or sponsor funding can enlarge that budget, but unpaid employee effort cannot.

Anything qualifying through none of these gets declined.

### The best operating alternative

Become the bookseller and curator, not the producer.

Have a library, school, university, employer, festival, or community organization own the venue, attendance, moderator, A/V, insurance, setup, and cleanup. You supply books and expertise—but only after a purchase order, minimum guarantee, or sufficient preorders exist.

The stronger rule is: **no event date exists until committed demand exists.**

### Most contrarian hypothesis

Your underpriced asset may be reader judgment, not event space.

Test whether publishers would pay for a consent-based “reader cabinet”: eight relevant local readers provide structured feedback on positioning, covers, discussion hooks, or launch messaging, receiving books and brief author access instead of attending a public event.

I’d estimate below a 35% chance of success—publisher procurement and small-sample credibility are serious obstacles—but it costs almost nothing to test and could turn unpaid publicity work into paid research. Never sell customer data; participation must be voluntary and reporting aggregated.

### Cheapest high-information experiment

**Hypothesis:** Eliminating routine store-produced events will free meaningful capacity without materially damaging the business, while at least some counterparties will pay for or produce the appearances they genuinely value.

**Critical assumptions:** Events create limited downstream value, and partners value worthwhile appearances enough to assume their true cost.

**Experiment:** Run two arms for 60 days:

- Freeze new store-produced bookings. Track what happens after the saved hours are redirected toward institutional sales, customer outreach, merchandising, ecommerce—or staff recovery.
- Give eight genuine event prospects only two choices: a partner-produced appearance with a minimum guarantee, or an offsite appearance activated by a purchase order or bulk commitment. Offer real terms. Compliments and “maybe” do not count.

**Resources:** About six owner-hours initially, 30 minutes of weekly tracking, a one-minute weekly staff strain rating, a spreadsheet, and no more than $50.

**Measure:** Staff-hours saved; contribution per total staff-hour; repeat-customer sales; qualified email or membership additions; named publisher/institutional relationships; staff strain; and binding partner commitments.

**Proposed success threshold:**

- At least 20 staff-hours saved
- Contribution per total staff-hour declines no more than 5%
- Repeat sales and qualified additions each decline no more than 10%
- No more than one named relationship materially weakens
- Average staff-strain score improves by at least two points
- At least two of eight prospects sign binding commitments meeting the 1.5× contribution-per-hour hurdle and requiring no more than 60 minutes of bookstore coordination

These percentages are decision thresholds, not industry facts. Adjust them for your normal volatility before starting, then lock them.

**Failure threshold:** Contribution per staff-hour falls over 10%, either customer metric falls over 15%, or two important relationships deteriorate without offsetting value. The partner model fails if zero or one prospect commits, none beats ordinary retail, or selling it consumes over eight hours.

**Learned either way:** Whether the calendar is valuable infrastructure or an inherited ritual—and whether other beneficiaries will fund it once you stop subsidizing them.

**If validated:** Keep only a capped, partner-produced slate.

**If falsified:** Restore only the particular function that deteriorated, then test a cheaper substitute. Don’t automatically restore the whole production apparatus.

### Ideas I would reject

- Redeemable tickets alone: useful, but production labor remains.
- Reader volunteers producing events: supervision and brand risk may simply relocate the work.
- Quarterly festivals: they batch tasks but concentrate disruption and downside.
- Event podcasts or video: they add an entirely new production problem.
- More promotion: it may improve attendance without repairing contribution per staff-hour.

What remains unknown is your own counterfactual: whether attendees subsequently buy more, return more often, join memberships, or generate valuable institutional relationships. If you cannot observe those effects, don’t allow vague “community value” to overrule visible staff exhaustion. Cultural programming can be worthwhile—but it should be an intentional investment with a budget, not an invisible tax on your team.
