You’re probably missing “calendar density,” not better turn-by-turn routing.

Route software can optimize the stops scheduled for Tuesday. It cannot fix a Tuesday containing customers from three towns, narrow appointment windows, scattered renewals, callbacks, and customers who selected their own dates.

At 900 quarterly accounts, you generate roughly 3,600 routine visits annually—about 72 per week over 50 working weeks. That should be dense enough if you reshape the schedule around geography.

What I’d change, in order:

1. **Create permanent micro-territories.** Divide each town into compact zones—not just three town-level territories. Give every zone a recurring service day or week, such as “northwest area, second Tuesday.” Assign technicians to contiguous zones and measure each zone separately.

2. **Schedule the neighborhood, not the individual customer.** Give customers a service window within their due month rather than preserving exact anniversary dates. Assign the date yourself, then notify them. Customer-selected dates and tight time windows are expensive routing constraints; optimization systems explicitly treat them as restrictions that can prevent efficient assignments. [OptimoRoute documentation](https://help.optimoroute.com/hc/en-us/articles/27712119329172-Intro-to-optimized-route-planning)

3. **Make routine quarterly work exterior-first and no-access-required where appropriate.** Automatically perform the exterior inspection/preventive service; handle interiors when requested or when monitoring indicates a need. This removes appointment-window and “nobody home” failures. Design this around product labels, state rules, your contract, and sound IPM practice: inspect, monitor, prevent, and treat based on findings. [EPA’s IPM framework](https://www.epa.gov/ipm/introduction-integrated-pest-management)

4. **Stop mixing routine work with random callbacks.** Reserve perhaps 10–15% of each zone day for callbacks and new starts within that same zone. A “quick” callback across town can break an otherwise productive route.

5. **Apply territory economics to sales and renewals.** Map every account by contribution margin after drive time. Reprice, convert, transfer, or decline isolated accounts. Then constrain marketing and door-knocking to neighborhoods adjacent to existing customers. Ten new accounts in one subdivision are worth much more operationally than ten spread across three towns.

6. **Remove non-route driving.** Separate windshield time into:
   - home/shop to first stop;
   - stop-to-stop;
   - supply or disposal runs;
   - lunch and administrative detours;
   - callbacks and reschedules.

   Route optimization primarily attacks the second category. Van restocking, remote paperwork, direct-to-first-stop policies, and clustered callbacks attack the others.

For the next four weeks, track only these measures by technician and micro-zone:

- Drive minutes per completed stop
- Completed stops per paid hour
- First-to-last-stop miles
- No-access/reschedule rate
- Callback minutes and miles
- Contribution margin per technician-day
- Percentage of stops outside the day’s designated zone

A strong operating rule would be: **no routine stop outside the assigned zone unless dispatch records an exception reason.**

If an eight-hour day currently includes 3.2 driving hours, reducing that to 2.0 hours releases 1.2 hours per technician-day—often enough for two or three more quarterly stops. The biggest lever is usually changing the customer promise from “pick your appointment” to “we service your neighborhood during this window and notify you beforehand.” That is a business-model change route software cannot make for you.
