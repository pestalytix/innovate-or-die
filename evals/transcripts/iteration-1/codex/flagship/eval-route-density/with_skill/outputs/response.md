The route optimizer is working on the wrong layer. You’re likely manufacturing travel through your account footprint, scheduling promises, and the assumption that every account deserves four individually scheduled truck rolls.

At 40% driving, this is probably a portfolio-design problem more than a sequencing problem.

## The important reframe

Stop optimizing “visits per day.” Optimize:

- Contribution margin per technician road-hour
- Pest-free account-months per truck roll
- Whether saved time eliminates overtime, defers a hire, adds stops, or removes an entire route-day

The real constraint is that licensed, label-compliant work must happen when biologically necessary. “Every home needs four scheduled visits” is likely an inherited product convention—not universally a biological requirement.

## The three strongest opportunities

1. Design the account book for density

Calculate profitability by account, micro-territory, and town after assigning drive time, service time, callbacks, and failed access.

Then:

- Stop selling into geographic gaps.
- Reprice, transfer, or eventually nonrenew route-orphan accounts.
- Give each town its own route P&L.
- Consider selling the weakest town’s book and buying a dense competitor route closer to your strongest territory.

This can feel wrong because it may reduce account count while increasing capacity and profit.

Kill this idea if the worst 10% of accounts cause less than 15% of road time—or removing them cannot eliminate a recurring capacity block.

2. Sell scheduling flexibility

Create two service promises:

- Base plan: service during a 7–10-day neighborhood period, exterior/no-one-home by default where permitted.
- Premium plan: customer-selected date or narrow window.

Customers who create route fragmentation should pay for precision. Everyone else supplies the flexibility that makes dense batching possible.

A useful pilot: offer this to 50 matched customers, with another 50 remaining on normal scheduling. Treat these as proposed decision rules, not industry benchmarks:

- Continue if at least 60% accept and road minutes per stop fall at least 20%.
- Stop if fewer than 40% accept or driving falls less than 10%.

3. Question the quarterly truck roll itself

For one legally eligible, low-risk pest/property cohort, test deeper onboarding—inspection, exclusion, risk correction, and monitoring—paired with an outcome warranty and evidence-triggered response.

The business model becomes “we keep pest pressure below the promised threshold,” rather than “we visibly visit four times.”

Test only one narrow cohort first. Compare 12 pilot homes with 12 matched quarterly accounts for 90 days. Stop if pest outcomes deteriorate, callbacks rise more than 10 percentage points, or total labor and warranty cost fails to decline meaningfully. Verify state rules, contracts, and product labels before enrolling anyone.

## Most contrarian hypothesis

You may need to shrink or trade your territory before growing again.

A bilateral exchange of outlier accounts with another licensed operator—or the sale of one structurally weak town—could create more value than acquiring another hundred scattered customers. Test this manually with anonymized geographic cells and three credible operators; don’t build a “route marketplace.”

## Cheapest high-information experiment

**Hypothesis:** A small minority of poorly positioned accounts consumes enough driving to create a removable capacity block.

**Critical assumption tested:** Road time is concentrated among route orphans rather than distributed evenly across the book.

**Experiment:** This week, export one complete 13-week service cycle. Attribute each driving leg to:

- Shop-to-first-stop and last-stop-to-shop
- Inter-stop travel
- Callbacks and schedule-recovery detours
- Supply runs
- Failed-access visits

Calculate road minutes, service minutes, callbacks, and gross margin by account and micro-territory. Then simulate transferring or removing the worst 10% while preserving viable clusters.

**Resources:** Six to eight owner/dispatcher hours, existing GPS and field-service data, and a spreadsheet. No incremental spend.

**Measured outcome:** Percentage of road minutes attributable to the worst 10%, plus whether their removal eliminates a recurring technician-day or other real capacity block.

**Success threshold:** At least 25% of road minutes and one recurring route-day eliminated.

**Failure threshold:** Less than 15% of road minutes and no capacity block eliminated.

**Learned either way:** Whether your primary constraint is portfolio topology or operating variability.

**If validated:** Test repricing or transferring a 20–30-account renewal cohort.

**If falsified:** Preserve the book and run the scheduling-flexibility pilot.

## Ideas I’d deprioritize

- Another optimizer: it cannot repair sparse demand or incompatible promises.
- Micro-depots: premature unless supply/depot legs are a major measured component.
- More dispatcher scorecards: measurement without pricing and renewal authority changes little.
- A dedicated callback technician: may merely exchange driving waste for idle payroll.
- A route-exchange platform: validate one bilateral trade first.

What’s still unknown—and should determine the answer—is the drive-time decomposition, density of each town, technician home locations, pest/service mix, customer access requirements, callbacks, margins, churn sensitivity, and applicable state and label restrictions. Without that, “40% driving” identifies the symptom but not which system created it.
