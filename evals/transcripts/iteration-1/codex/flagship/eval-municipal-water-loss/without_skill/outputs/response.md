A 22% NRW rate is only a starting signal. NRW includes physical leakage, apparent losses such as meter or billing errors, and authorized but unbilled use. The utility should therefore avoid treating all 22% as leaking pipe. EPA and AWWA recommend beginning with a validated water balance and using separate indicators for real and apparent losses. [EPA guidance](https://nepis.epa.gov/Exe/ZyPURL.cgi?Dockey=P1009VCZ.TXT), [AWWA Water Loss Control](https://www.awwa.org/resource/water-loss-control/)

“No capital expenditure” should mean using existing staff, equipment, valves, meters and SCADA, although some measures will consume operating and maintenance funds.

| Operating approach | Evidence that would confirm it works—or is worth pursuing | Evidence that would refute or deprioritize it |
|---|---|---|
| Validate the water balance | Production-meter checks, billing extracts and timing reconciliation materially change the estimated NRW volume or improve audit data-validity scores. | Independent checks reproduce the 22% estimate within meter uncertainty and show good data completeness. |
| Audit billing and customer records | Samples reveal wrong multipliers, unit conversions, duplicate/missing accounts, skipped reads, estimated bills, inactive accounts using water, or meter readings not transferred to billing. Corrected accounts increase billed volume without a comparable rise in system input. | A statistically credible account sample has negligible errors and billing-system totals reconcile with meter-reading totals. |
| Target stopped or under-registering customer meters using existing testing capacity | Bench or field tests—stratified by meter age, size, make and consumption—show significant negative registration bias; correcting installations, reads or billing produces sustained revenue recovery. | Test results fall within accuracy tolerances and suspect cohorts bill similarly to comparable meters. Meter replacement itself should wait for a capital program unless it qualifies as routine O&M. |
| Control unauthorized consumption | Field inspections and consumption-pattern analysis find bypasses, tampered meters, illegal connections or undocumented hydrant use; enforcement produces a measurable increase in billed consumption or reduction in zone flow. | Inspections find few violations, and recovered volume is too small to justify continuing the effort. |
| Measure and manage authorized unbilled uses | Logging flushing, firefighting, utility facilities, parks and municipal accounts closes a material portion of the water balance; operational changes reduce these volumes or policy changes convert them to billed use. | Verified authorized use is immaterial. Merely reclassifying or billing it improves reported NRW or revenue but does not save water, which should be reported explicitly. |
| Analyze existing zone meters and SCADA for minimum-night flow | Persistent night flows exceed credible customer night use; particular zones show abnormal step changes or flows that decline after leak repair. | Night flow is explained by legitimate continuous users, input-meter uncertainty or boundary-valve movements, with no residual leakage signal. Do not build new DMAs under the no-capital constraint. |
| Intensify leak detection with existing crews and equipment | Acoustic surveys, customer reports and night-flow analysis find previously hidden leaks; verified flow drops after isolation or repair. Track gallons recovered per crew-hour. | Repeated surveys find little recoverable leakage, or verified savings cost more in O&M than avoided treatment and marginal supply costs. |
| Shorten leak awareness, location and repair times | Work-order records show long delays or repeat repairs. After revising dispatch, escalation, crew scheduling and materials handling, average leak duration and estimated loss per event decline. | Response times are already short, repairs rarely recur, and leakage volume shows no relationship to repair delay. Speed and quality of repair are recognized core leakage controls. [World Bank NRW tools](https://ppp.worldbank.org/water-and-sanitation/performance-based-contracting-tools-non-revenue-water-reduction-tools-resources) |
| Optimize pressure using existing pumps, PRVs, tanks and controls | Pressure logging shows excess pressure, especially overnight. A controlled trial preserves minimum service and fire-flow requirements while reducing night flow, leak frequency or burst rate. | Pressures are already at operational minima, customers lose adequate service, or leakage does not respond materially. Avoid indiscriminate pressure reduction. |
| Stop tank overflows and improve storage operations | Level/flow records, alarms or site observations document overflows; revised setpoints, pump schedules or operator procedures eliminate them. | No overflows occur, or estimated overflow volume is negligible. |
| Improve valve and operating discipline | Tests identify open cross-boundary valves, incorrect valve status, pressure transients or abnormal pump cycling; corrected procedures reduce unexplained flow or bursts. | Field valve positions match records and transient/flow analysis finds no operational contribution. |
| Establish weekly NRW accountability | Named owners, zone exception reports and verified “water saved/revenue recovered” measures sustain reductions after individual interventions. | Activity counts rise—surveys, inspections, repairs—but validated loss volumes and repair recurrence do not improve. |

For the first 90 days, I would prioritize: validate the water balance; reconcile every connection from meter read through billing; rank zones using existing night-flow and pressure data; clear the highest-loss repair backlog; and run one controlled pressure trial. Evaluate results in volume—such as gallons per connection per day and real loss per mile—not only NRW percentage, because the percentage can improve simply when billed consumption rises. Collection of unpaid bills may improve cash flow, but it does not reduce NRW if that consumption was already correctly billed.
