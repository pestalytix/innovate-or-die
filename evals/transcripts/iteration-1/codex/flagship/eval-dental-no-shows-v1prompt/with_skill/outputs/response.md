The best-supported move is not another generic reminder. It is targeted escalation: contact high-risk hygiene patients earlier, then use automated voice or a brief staff call when they do not respond.

My confidence labels are judgmental, not statistically estimated. They reflect the strength of the research and how indirectly medical-clinic findings transfer to dental hygiene.

## Most important reframing

The practice probably has an early-information problem, not simply a forgetting problem. The remaining 14% likely combines unreachable patients, stale six-month commitments, practical barriers, and patients who silently abandon appointments.

Measure separately:

- True no-shows: no attendance and no usable advance notice.
- Chair utilization: completed hygiene visits divided by available hygiene capacity.

A cancellation workflow can improve the first number without improving production unless released appointments are refilled.

## Top opportunities

| Option | Expected effect | Confidence |
|---|---:|---|
| Risk-targeted earlier SMS plus IVR/live-call escalation | Best candidate; plausibly 1–3 percentage points practice-wide | **High** that it helps somewhat; **moderate** that it exceeds 2 points |
| One-tap early release plus filtered standby list | Likely converts some no-shows into cancellations; utilization improves only when refilled | **Moderate** for earlier notice; **low–moderate** for direct no-show reduction |
| Shorter booking horizon or nearer-term renewal | Potentially large if long-lead appointments dominate misses | **Low–moderate** because lead time is a strong predictor but causal evidence is weaker |

### 1. Risk-targeted escalation

Use prior no-show/late-cancellation history, long booking lead time, failed message delivery, and nonresponse—not demographics—to identify risk. Send an actionable message 5–7 days ahead, then escalate unresolved cases 72–24 hours ahead through IVR or a short staff call.

This is the conventional winner. Trials found that multiple reminders outperformed one reminder, while targeted live calls produced larger reductions in high-risk populations already receiving automation. The exact dental-hygiene effect remains uncertain because most incremental evidence comes from medical settings. [Multiple-reminder RCT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30130032/), [risk-targeted outreach RCT](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5130951/), [added IVR trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42418615/).

Biggest failure mode: staff call time exceeds the value of recovered visits, especially if patients face barriers a reminder cannot solve.

### 2. Frictionless release plus standby recovery

Change the SMS to offer three one-tap choices: keep, release, or reschedule. Test specific opportunity-cost wording such as: “Releasing by 5 p.m. lets us offer this appointment to someone waiting.” Route releases immediately to a list filtered by visit length, clinical eligibility, insurance, and stated short-notice availability.

I have **moderate confidence** it will convert some silent misses into earlier cancellations. I have only **low–moderate confidence** it will reduce the underlying propensity to miss. Confirmation by itself has weak evidence; opportunity-cost wording has performed better in randomized messaging studies. [SMS wording trials](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137306).

Biggest failure mode: the practice records fewer no-shows but completes no additional visits because releases arrive too late or the standby list is weak.

### 3. Shorter-horizon renewal

For a bounded segment—initially patients with long booking horizons and prior misses—record the clinical due date and preferences, but renew or allocate the exact appointment 2–4 weeks before care.

The contrarian hypothesis is that part of the 14% is created by asking patients to make precise commitments months before they know their future constraints. Long lead time consistently predicts nonattendance, but evidence that open-access scheduling causally fixes it is heterogeneous. [Determinants review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0168851018300459), [open-access review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11231932/).

Confidence is **low–moderate**. Pilot narrowly: it fails if completed hygiene visits per 100 due patients decline, even when the recorded no-show rate improves.

## Cheapest high-information experiment

**Hypothesis:** High-risk appointments receiving a 7-day actionable SMS plus IVR/live escalation will have fewer true no-shows than those receiving the current 48-hour SMS alone.

**Critical assumption tested:** A meaningful share of residual no-shows is reachable and reveals or changes plans early enough to recover the appointment.

**Experiment:** Randomize the next 100 high-risk hygiene appointments 1:1. Keep existing reminders in control; add the escalation ladder in treatment. This is a feasibility test, not a powered efficacy trial.

**Resources:** Existing messaging tools, roughly two setup hours, and recorded staff minutes. Include actual labor and vendor charges rather than imposing an unsupported budget.

**Measured outcome:** Successful contacts, actionable releases/reschedules, true no-shows, completed visits, refills, and staff minutes per targeted appointment.

**Success threshold:** At least 15% of escalated nonresponders provide actionable information, protocol adherence is at least 90%, and outreach cost per recovered visit is below that visit’s contribution margin. The 15% threshold is a predeclared business decision threshold, not a published effect estimate.

**Failure threshold:** Fewer than 5% provide actionable information, adherence falls below 90%, or recovery costs exceed contribution margin.

**Learned either way:** Whether escalation reaches the right people, whether earlier information is operationally usable, and whether a larger outcome trial is worth conducting.

**If validated:** Run a sufficiently powered trial. Detecting a decrease from 14% to 11% conventionally requires roughly 1,900 appointments per arm; duration equals 3,800 divided by weekly eligible hygiene volume.

**If falsified:** Stop intensifying reminders and investigate the longest-lead segment with a renewal pilot and structured interviews.

## Compact kill list

- Deposits or no-show fees: **low confidence**; the strongest randomized fee trial found no improvement.
- Blanket transportation offers: **low confidence** absent evidence that transportation drives this practice’s misses.
- More reminder texts for everyone: **moderate confidence** in a small effect, but less efficient than targeting.
- Confirmation alone: **low confidence**; “YES” is not a strong commitment signal.
- Predictive overbooking: **high confidence it can protect utilization**, but **high confidence it does not reduce no-show behavior** and it risks waits and overtime.

Before implementation, audit six months of appointments by prior misses, lead time, time slot, provider, reminder delivery, and late-cancellation coding. That decomposition could change the ranking—especially if invalid contact data or long booking horizons explain most of the 14%.
