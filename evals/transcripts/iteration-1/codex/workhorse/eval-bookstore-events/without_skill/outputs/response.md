I wouldn’t drop them outright yet. I’d stop treating them as standalone profit centers and run a short, disciplined test: keep only events that clearly support one of three goals—book sales, new-customer acquisition, or community/brand value.

For the next 3–6 months:

- Cut low-fit events: prioritize authors with a local following, strong preorder potential, or a partner who brings an audience.
- Require preorders or tickets for most events—e.g., ticket includes the book, store credit, or a drink. This reduces no-shows and protects staff time.
- Get a sponsor or partner: libraries, universities, publishers, local businesses, and venues can supply space, promotion, or underwriting.
- Standardize operations: one event format, fixed staffing model, clear author/publisher responsibilities, and a minimum sales/preorder threshold.
- Track the real cost: staff hours, setup/cleanup, inventory risk, and opportunity cost—not just event-night revenue. Also track new email signups and repeat visits.

Then make events an intentional product: perhaps fewer, better events—say one or two “anchor” events a month—rather than a constant calendar.

My rule: keep events that either break even after fully loaded labor, or reliably create enough long-term customer value to justify a measured subsidy. If neither is true after the test, discontinue them without guilt and redirect that energy into higher-return community tactics: book clubs, paid workshops, school partnerships, signed-stock drops, and strong preorder campaigns.
