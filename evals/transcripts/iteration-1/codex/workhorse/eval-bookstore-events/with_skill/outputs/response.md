I wouldn’t drop author events entirely. I’d stop running them as default programming.

**My strongest recommendation:** pause regular events for 60–90 days and relaunch only under a three-lane policy:

1. **Mission events** — a small, explicit annual community budget. These may lose money on purpose; cap them.
2. **Sales events** — require enough prepaid book orders, ticket-book bundles, or a publisher/author guarantee to cover *fully loaded* labor, not just cash expenses.
3. **Underwritten events** — a library, school, university, local business, or civic group pays for the community benefit they receive.

Everything else becomes a signed-stock drop, short in-store appearance, or a partner-hosted event. The key deletion: an author visit does not have to mean you operate a live event.

The reframing is that your scarce asset isn’t “an event calendar.” It’s local reader trust, a book-buying audience, and a credible stage. Right now, the publisher/author captures much of the attention and goodwill while you absorb the staffing cost. The payer should change.

Top opportunities:

- **Pre-sold launch threshold.** Confirm an event only once it has, say, a set number of prepaid books or a revenue minimum calculated from your actual staff hours. Readers get admission with book purchase or a redeemable deposit.  
  - Why it matters: demand is proven before you schedule labor.  
  - Failure risk: customers resist prepaying; then the event was unlikely to produce dependable sales anyway.

- **Sell “reader access,” not a room.** Offer a simple package to publishers, schools, libraries, and companies: staff-led conversation, curated table, author handling, book ordering, and promotion. Make the partner the underwriter.  
  - Why non-obvious: it turns programming from retail overhead into a service the beneficiary buys.  
  - Failure risk: partners may have constrained budgets or prefer their own venues.

- **Neighborhood reading salons.** Instead of hosting every author, equip 5–10 customer hosts with a discussion kit and bulk-order link; the author joins one virtual Q&A or signs stock.  
  - Why it matters: one staff setup can produce multiple small buying occasions, without a single crowded night.  
  - Biggest risk: it requires finding enthusiastic hosts.

Most contrarian hypothesis: the best “event program” may be an intentionally tiny, prestigious one—perhaps six mission-defining events a year—while the rest of your author relationship becomes preorders, signed copies, and partner-funded salons. A fuller calendar can make the store feel culturally active while quietly consuming its best resource: staff attention.

Run this cheap test before making a permanent decision:

> **Hypothesis:** At least some authors/partners will accept a pre-sale or underwriting rule, and those events will cover fully loaded labor.  
> **Critical assumption tested:** The market values your audience enough to share the cost.  
> **Experiment:** For the next three author requests, offer only two formats: (a) event activates at your preset preorder threshold, or (b) partner/publisher underwriting. Offer signed-stock drops if neither works.  
> **Resources:** One manager’s hour to calculate your real event cost; a standard email/form; no new spend.  
> **Measure:** Number accepting, preorders before confirmation, and actual staff hours.  
> **Success threshold:** Two of three meet the threshold or secure underwriting.  
> **Failure threshold:** Zero do.  
> **Learned either way:** Whether the issue is event execution or that the market will not pay for the value it asks you to provide.  
> **If validated:** Make it the standard policy.  
> **If falsified:** Keep only your capped mission events and discontinue routine live programming.

A compact kill list:

- “Just market events better” — treats demand as the problem when labor economics may be.
- “Add a membership program” — potentially useful, but too indirect to fix per-event staffing.
- “Use volunteers” — can shift burden and create reliability risk, not change the model.
- “Livestream every event” — adds production work unless it meaningfully changes who pays.
- “Run more events to build momentum” — dangerous if each incremental event consumes scarce staff capacity.

The missing number is your **fully loaded event cost**: staff hours, setup/cleanup, interruption to normal selling, extra security/AV, and the sales floor opportunity cost. “Barely breaks even” may already mean it loses money. Calculate that once, set a firm threshold, and let the policy—not the awkwardness of saying no—make the decision.
