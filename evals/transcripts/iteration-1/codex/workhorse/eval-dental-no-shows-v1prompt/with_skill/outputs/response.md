The most likely winner is a targeted “confirm or release” workflow—not another universal reminder.

Your 48-hour SMS already addresses forgetting. The remaining no-shows are more likely driven by unresolved conflicts, long lead times, and patients who never made a real commitment. Require a one-tap confirmation or reschedule; place non-confirmers into a short staff-call queue, prioritized by prior no-shows and long booking lead times. Release unresolved slots early enough to refill.

Confidence: moderately high (about 70%) that this reduces the practice’s 14% hygiene no-show rate materially—likely by converting no-shows into earlier cancellations and refills, not magically making every patient attend. Prior no-shows and long lead times are consistently important predictors, and targeted phone outreach has stronger evidence than adding texts. [Predictor review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29482948/), [targeted intervention review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36508503/)

The key reframing: optimize **kept-or-refilled chair time**, not the reported no-show percentage. A strict confirmation policy can make the rate look better simply by removing appointments; it only creates value if the released hygiene time is actually rebooked.

Top options:

1. **Risk-targeted call + one-click confirm/reschedule**
   - Mechanism: call only patients who do not confirm and have risk signals (prior missed hygiene, appointment booked far ahead, perhaps certain time/day patterns). The call asks one question: “Are we keeping this, or should I find a better time?”
   - Confidence: **70%**. Best evidence-backed next move; in broader outpatient evidence, prediction-targeted calls outperformed routine reminder approaches, though the exact dental effect is uncertain. [Review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36508503/)
   - Biggest failure mode: staff make generic reminder calls rather than resolving the scheduling barrier.

2. **Move recall from “book six months ahead” to “reserve, then activate”**
   - Mechanism: for patients with a no-show history, do not treat a distant checkout booking as firm. Hold a preferred window and invite them to select/confirm 2–4 weeks before due date.
   - Confidence: **55%** that it lowers no-shows; **only 40%** that it improves total hygiene production unless there is a disciplined recall process. Long booking lead time is a recurrent no-show driver, but this can turn missed visits into overdue unbooked patients. [Review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29482948/)
   - Use it only for the high-risk segment, not everyone.

3. **Build a true short-notice hygiene fill list**
   - Mechanism: when a non-confirmed visit is released, send a targeted “tomorrow/this week” opening message to patients due or overdue for hygiene, with a one-tap claim link.
   - Confidence: **65%** that it reduces lost chair time; **45%** that it reduces the raw no-show rate directly.
   - This is especially valuable in a six-operatory practice because it makes the confirmation policy economically safe.

Most contrarian hypothesis worth testing: stop automatically scheduling the next hygiene visit six months out for repeat no-show patients. Treat the appointment as a scarce allocation that must be actively claimed closer to its date. It may feel like reducing service, but it may increase completed hygiene visits if the present system creates nominal appointments rather than genuine commitments.

Cheapest high-information experiment:

- Hypothesis: non-confirming, higher-risk hygiene patients can be converted or released early enough to refill, reducing no-shows by at least 30% in that group.
- Critical assumption tested: a human barrier-resolution call changes behavior beyond the existing SMS.
- Experiment: for four weeks, flag hygiene appointments with prior no-show or booking lead time over 60 days. Send the normal 48-hour SMS with Confirm/Reschedule. Call only non-confirmers at 36–24 hours; release unresolved appointments at the agreed cutoff to a fill list.
- Resources: one scheduling staff member; roughly 5–15 calls per day depending on volume; no new platform required if the PMS can tag outcomes.
- Measure: no-show rate, cancellation lead time, and % of released slots refilled—compared with the same weekday/time blocks over the prior 4–8 weeks.
- Success threshold: at least a 30% relative reduction in no-shows for the flagged group, with at least half of released slots refilled.
- Failure threshold: under 15% relative reduction, or fewer than 25% of released slots refilled.
- If validated: expand to all hygiene non-confirmers and improve the fill-list automation.
- If falsified: test the recall-window model next, rather than piling on more reminders.

I would not prioritize: a second generic SMS (low incremental confidence; reminder timing within 1–7 days has not reliably changed attendance), blanket deposits or penalties (uncertain effect and patient-friction risk), blanket overbooking (protects capacity but does not reduce no-shows), or an ML project before proving that the clinic will act differently on a simple risk list. [Reminder evidence synthesis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25642537/)
