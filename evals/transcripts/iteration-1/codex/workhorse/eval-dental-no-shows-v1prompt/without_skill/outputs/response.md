The best next move is a **two-way confirmation-and-release workflow**, not simply more generic SMS reminders.

Send a concise SMS at 48–72 hours requiring a one-tap reply: “Reply C to keep, R to reschedule.” For unconfirmed hygiene visits, send a final 24-hour prompt; have staff call only high-risk/unconfirmed patients, then release the slot early enough to fill from a short-notice list. Make cancel/reschedule frictionless in every message.

**Confidence: high.** Reminder systems consistently reduce missed appointments, but your practice already has the basic version. The evidence suggests the incremental gain comes from turning reminders into a usable cancellation/rescheduling and re-allocation process; no-show risk also concentrates among patients with prior misses and unconfirmed appointments. [Systematic review of reminder systems](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27110102/), [review of prediction-targeted interventions](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36508503/).

My priority order:

1. **Two-way confirmation + unconfirmed-patient escalation + waitlist fill** — **high confidence**  
   Most likely to reduce true no-shows *and* recover capacity. Track confirmations, late cancellations, no-shows, and reclaimed slots separately; otherwise a “lower no-show rate” can just be no-shows relabeled as late cancellations.

2. **Target live calls to patients with prior no-shows, unconfirmed visits, long booking lead times, or unreliable contact details** — **moderate-high confidence**  
   Calls cost more than SMS, and average effects are similar, but targeted calls are sensible when used selectively rather than for every hygiene patient. [Cochrane review](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD007458_mobile-phone-messaging-reminders-attendance-healthcare-appointments).

3. **Improve booking commitment: schedule before the patient leaves, verify mobile number/preferred channel, calendar link, and clear rescheduling path** — **moderate confidence**  
   This addresses poor contact data and forgetfulness, but direct dental evidence on each feature is thinner than for reminders themselves.

4. **Offer a short-notice list and modest flexibility for recurring-hygiene patients** — **moderate confidence**  
   This may not prevent all absences, but it materially reduces lost chair time and makes rescheduling easier.

5. **Late-cancel/no-show fee or card-on-file policy, with hardship exceptions** — **low-to-moderate confidence for reducing no-shows; higher risk**  
   It can change behavior, but strong comparative evidence is limited and it can deter care or disproportionately burden patients facing transport, work, or financial barriers. Use only after fixing the confirmation/rescheduling process, communicate it clearly, and apply consistently.

6. **Adding more reminder texts or moving the existing reminder from 48 to 24 hours** — **low confidence as the main answer**  
   Evidence does not show a reliably superior reminder timing within roughly 1–7 days; another generic reminder alone is unlikely to be the largest lever. [TURNUP evidence synthesis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25642537/).

A practical 60-day test: use the new workflow for hygiene appointments, compare against the prior 8 weeks, and aim first to cut 14% to roughly 9–11%. That target is plausible, but I’d treat any exact percentage estimate as uncertain until you see your own confirmation and recovery data.
