The strongest hypothesis is conditional: early churn is not primarily a documentation problem if customers lack a near-term, externally required project outcome that makes use inevitable. Confirm that first. If true, redesign activation around one accepted project artifact—not around learning the app.

**Reframing**

Facts: churn is 6% monthly and concentrated in the first 90 days; onboarding is a 45-minute call plus docs.

Unknowns that matter: product category, trade, churn reasons, next-project timing, substitutes, and whether a completed artifact predicts retention. Do not assume all early churn has one cause—separate cash-flow/nonpayment, wrong ICP, no live project, product gaps, and implementation failures.

The likely inherited constraints are “every account gets onboarded,” “seats are the unit of value,” and “customers must log into our product before getting value.”

**Top opportunities**

1. **Make one compulsory project artifact the product’s activation unit**

   Pick one artifact customers must submit or have accepted within 30–60 days—potentially a payment, compliance, change-order, or closeout packet, depending on the product. Ingest only the minimum existing inputs and produce a permissioned, auditable submission flow via links, email, or their existing system.

   The mechanism: a deadline and accepted submission create immediate economic value; optional product learning does not. Document AI, SMS, and integrations are supporting mechanics, not the strategy.

   Radical version: become an embedded/API artifact service with little or no standalone workspace.

   It fails if incumbents already handle the artifact well, or it takes more than ~15 minutes of human/vendor effort to create a correct one.

2. **Turn ICP and pricing into retention architecture**

   Only sell an activation promise when there is a named sponsor and a qualifying live project with a milestone inside 45 days. Offer a narrowly bounded first-project outcome: fixed implementation fee, or a 90-day fee at risk until the named artifact is submitted and accepted. Avoid guaranteeing legal or compliance correctness.

   The mechanism: it rejects accounts structurally unable to activate, and puts vendor effort on the event that could form a durable habit.

   This is not just better onboarding; it changes the commercial contract from “access to software” to “a verified operational result.”

   It fails if milestone completion does not produce materially better month-4 retention, or delivery labor consumes first-year gross profit.

3. **Test a GC/owner-funded network model**

   The contrarian option: sell a GC or owner a required workflow covering five or more subcontractors. The GC pays because it receives coordination/compliance value; subs participate because the workflow is tied to a real project requirement, not another software subscription.

   This could reposition the company as cross-company project infrastructure. Its biggest risk is that it becomes duplicate work beside Procore, Autodesk, email, or existing portals—and that subs perceive it as surveillance.

   Kill it quickly if fewer than 3 of 10 target GCs identify a recurring costly workflow and will sponsor a paid pilot.

**Most contrarian hypothesis**

The best customer may not be the subcontractor at all. The subcontractor can be the user and data contributor, while the economically motivated payer is the GC, owner, surety, payroll/compliance intermediary, or another party harmed by missing/late records. That is worth testing, not assuming.

**Cheapest high-information experiment**

Hypothesis: for qualified accounts, completing one externally accepted artifact through the product causes higher 120-day retention than comparable qualified accounts without that intervention.

Critical assumption tested: the artifact is genuinely required, can be produced cheaply, and its acceptance leads to repeat use rather than a one-off services deliverable.

Experiment: this week, analyze recent cohorts by time-to-next-project and first completed workflow. Interview 10 retainers/churners about the exact artifact and substitute they used. Recruit 5 qualified accounts with a named artifact due within 45 days; manually run the workflow. Compare them with matched qualified historical accounts or a small holdout group.

Resources: product lead + CS lead, ~20 hours, 10 interviews, 5 pilot offers; no major build.

Measured outcome: paid-pilot conversion, minutes of vendor labor per artifact, correction rate, acceptance by the named recipient, repeat workflow usage, and 90/120-day retention versus matched accounts.

Success threshold: at least 3 paid or purchase-committed pilots; under 15 minutes of vendor effort per artifact; at least 90% accepted without material correction; and a pre-agreed retention/continued-use lift versus matches.

Failure threshold: fewer than 2 commitments, over 30 minutes per artifact, under 80% acceptance, or no retention/continued-use signal. Stop the generalized build.

Before collecting project documents, run a security/authority gate: who may provide them, required retention/audit rules, contractual data restrictions, and whether the chosen artifact carries payment, lien, certified-payroll, signature, or professional-liability implications.

Run two parallel lightweight tests:

- Outcome-priced model: offer the bounded outcome to five qualified prospects; proceed only if delivery cost can plausibly remain below first-year gross profit and the outcome predicts retention.
- GC-funded model: interview 10 target GCs with one named workflow; proceed only if at least three will sponsor a paid pilot involving five or more subcontractors.

**Conventional improvements still worth doing**

Use these only for accounts with a qualifying live project: role-specific setup, a pre-populated first project instead of a blank workspace, a single “first accepted outcome” checklist, and proactive support triggered by stalled project events—not generic training cadence.

**Kill list**

- Longer contracts: changes reported churn, not customer value.
- Cancellation friction: risks distrust and bad referrals.
- Generic concierge onboarding: can conceal a services business.
- AI document extraction alone: creates a workspace, not a compelling outcome.
- Broad benchmarking: needs scale and does little for a day-90 activation failure.

What may still be missing is the actual substitute customers return to—spreadsheet, email chain, GC portal, accounting system, or paper process. That answer should determine the artifact and architecture, not the other way around.
