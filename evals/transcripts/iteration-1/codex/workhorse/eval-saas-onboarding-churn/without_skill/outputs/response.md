Early churn usually means customers bought before they had a reliable path to first value. Improve onboarding, but also address fit, incentives, product integration, and commercial design.

- Define a 90-day “activation” outcome, not just onboarding completion. For example: first project set up, required documents submitted, bid/invoice workflow completed, and at least two active users returning weekly. Track activation by cohort and correlate each milestone with retention.

- Replace the single 45-minute call with a 30/60/90-day success program:
  - Pre-call implementation checklist: job types, team roles, existing tools, sample project.
  - Guided setup focused on one live workflow rather than a product tour.
  - Scheduled follow-ups at days 7, 21, and 45, with a named customer-success owner.
  - Triggered outreach when usage drops or a key activation step stalls.

- Make onboarding construction-native. Offer role-specific paths for owners, office managers, project managers, and field users; use templates for common subcontractor workflows such as COIs, lien waivers, change orders, compliance, payroll, or invoicing—depending on the product.

- Reduce the “empty account” problem. Seed accounts with templates, example projects, default approval flows, import tools, and guided checklists. If customers must manually enter lots of data before value appears, early churn will persist.

- Add a concierge implementation tier. For smaller subcontractors, a lightweight “we configure it with you” service may outperform self-serve documentation. This can be bundled into annual plans or sold as paid implementation, depending on willingness to pay.

- Change qualification and packaging. Identify churn-heavy segments—e.g., very small firms, low project volume, customers without an office administrator, or customers lacking an adjacent system—and adjust:
  - Tighten qualification or disqualify poor-fit accounts.
  - Create a simpler, lower-commitment package for low-complexity firms.
  - Sell a more supported package for customers needing implementation help.
  - Align sales messaging with the first achievable value, avoiding oversold capabilities.

- Shift commercial incentives toward retained adoption. Pay a portion of sales commission after 90-day activation or retention, and compensate customer success on activated accounts rather than calls completed. This improves handoff quality and discourages poor-fit deals.

- Offer annual or staged commitments carefully. Annual contracts can reduce logo churn mechanically, but they should be paired with real implementation support and clear value checkpoints. A 90-day paid pilot that converts into annual service can be better than a low-friction monthly plan that invites abandonment.

- Integrate into existing systems instead of asking users to maintain another silo. For construction subcontractors, integrations with accounting, payroll, project management, document storage, and general-contractor portals can turn the product into part of daily operations. Prioritize based on the systems used by retained customers, not just the most-requested integration.

- Re-architect around the daily workflow. If the product is mainly a destination users must remember to visit, consider event-driven architecture:
  - Ingest project/accounting data automatically.
  - Generate alerts, tasks, and approvals in email, SMS, Teams/Slack, or mobile.
  - Sync status back to systems of record.
  - Provide APIs/webhooks for larger subcontractors.
  
  The goal is to make the product operational infrastructure, not a separate dashboard.

- Create network or ecosystem value. If value increases when general contractors, suppliers, insurers, or compliance partners participate, build controlled sharing and collaboration features. This creates switching costs and makes the platform useful even when internal usage is light.

- Introduce proactive risk detection. Use product signals—no second user invited, no live project, no integrations connected, missing setup steps, declining weekly activity—to score churn risk. Trigger human outreach, in-app interventions, or a simplified workflow before day 30, not after cancellation.

A practical first sequence:

1. Analyze the last 6–12 months of churn by segment, sales source, use case, activation milestones, and time-to-first-value.  
2. Choose one retained-customer behavior that must happen in the first 14 days.  
3. Test a high-touch 90-day implementation motion for the highest-value segment.  
4. In parallel, build the product/integration changes that remove manual setup and embed the workflow in customers’ existing systems.  
5. Update qualification, pricing, and sales incentives once the churn pattern is clear.

The key distinction: onboarding improvements help customers learn the product; business-model and architecture changes make the product easier to adopt, harder to abandon, and better aligned with how subcontractors actually run work.
