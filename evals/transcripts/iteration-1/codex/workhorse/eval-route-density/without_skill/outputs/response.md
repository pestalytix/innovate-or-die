You’re likely missing a *service-design* problem, not another routing problem. Route software can optimize the stops you give it; it can’t fix an account base that is geographically scattered or scheduled in the wrong cadence.

At 900 quarterly accounts, the big levers are:

- **Measure drive time by town, ZIP/neighborhood, and technician—not just overall.** Map every account and identify the thin “outlier” clusters. A handful of low-density areas can consume an absurd share of labor.
- **Schedule by geography first, due-date window second.** Give each neighborhood a designated service week each quarter. Customers get “service during the week of…” rather than a precise recurring day. This is usually the largest operational change.
- **Build fixed territories / route cells.** Each tech should repeatedly own compact zones, with minimal cross-town handoffs. Avoid dispatching based mainly on who has room that day.
- **Set minimum-density rules for new sales.** Charge a travel surcharge, offer a waitlist/service day, or decline isolated accounts outside defined route cells. Otherwise sales keeps creating expensive windshield time.
- **Clean up legacy outliers.** For accounts far from a dense cluster: reprice, move to a specific low-frequency route day, convert to annual/seasonal, refer out, or non-renew. Losing a few unprofitable accounts may create capacity for far more profitable ones.
- **Separate callbacks and new starts from production routes.** Same-day “squeeze-ins” destroy density. Reserve blocks or assign a rotating flex tech/day.
- **Use renewal timing to reset schedules.** Don’t try to move 900 customers at once. As each account renews, migrate it to its neighborhood’s service week.
- **Examine the service model itself.** If quarterly visits are short and predictable, routes should feel like postal routes. If each visit has variable scope, customer appointment preferences may be creating the driving problem. Standardizing visit scope and communications helps.

A useful target metric is **revenue and stops per drive-hour**, by route cell. Once you see that, decisions become clearer: “This neighborhood needs 18 more accounts to justify a dedicated day,” versus “these six accounts need repricing or reassignment.”

The uncomfortable but common conclusion: at 40% drive time, you may need to deliberately trade a little customer scheduling flexibility—and possibly a few fringe accounts—for much higher route density. Route software then becomes genuinely effective instead of merely reducing the damage.
