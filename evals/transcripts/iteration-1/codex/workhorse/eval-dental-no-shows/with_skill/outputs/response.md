The strongest fix is to stop treating hygiene appointments as calendar entries that need reminding, and instead manage them as scarce, revocable capacity: patients must either recommit to the slot or release it early enough for the practice to use it.

A 48-hour one-way reminder is often too late and asks nothing of the patient. Build a two-track attendance system:

- 7 days before: text a one-tap choice—“Keep my time” or “I need to change it.”
- 48 hours before: only nonresponders receive a short personal call/text from the hygiene coordinator. Do not merely resend the reminder.
- A “release” action should be frictionless and positively framed: “Can’t make it? Tell us now and we’ll protect your priority for a future hygiene visit.” This turns a no-show into usable inventory.
- Maintain a short-notice list segmented by time-of-day availability. When a slot is released, offer it sequentially by text; the first confirmed patient gets it.
- Patients with repeated late cancellations/no-shows move from protected recurring slots to a “confirm-to-hold” tier until reliability improves. Avoid blanket penalties; allow staff discretion for hardship, illness, and transport issues.

The key reframing: the target is not “more reminder messages.” It is **recovered hygiene-chair hours**:

`attended + released early and refilled`, divided by total booked hygiene slots.

Three opportunities worth testing:

1. **Protected recurring-slot membership**

   Give reliable recall patients a valuable recurring “home” appointment—e.g., Tuesday at 8:00 every six months. They retain it by confirming and attending; repeated misses release it to other patients.

   Mechanism: patients protect something they perceive as scarce and personally useful, rather than ignoring a generic appointment. The risk is that it can feel punitive, so explain it as priority access, not discipline.

2. **Reward the early release, not just attendance**

   Offer a small benefit for releasing 24+ hours ahead—priority rebooking, preferred times, or a modest practice-defined loyalty credit where appropriate. Do not reward late releases.

   Mechanism: patients who already know they will miss have a reason to notify you while the slot is still fillable. The non-obvious point is that a tiny benefit can be cheaper than an empty hygiene hour. Guard against gaming with a clear limit and no benefit for repeat patterns.

3. **Build a recovery queue instead of casually overbooking**

   Create a living list of patients who want earlier appointments, sorted by availability window and likely treatment value/clinical appropriateness. Use released slots to fill from this queue. Keep a small block of useful fallback work—recall outreach, perio education, same-day limited exams—rather than routinely double-booking hygiene.

   Mechanism: a no-show becomes a fast matching problem. Overbooking may improve utilization, but it can create long waits and degrade care when everyone arrives.

Most contrarian hypothesis: a small, carefully designed **“give us notice” reward** will recover more chair time than escalating reminder frequency, because the decisive behavior is often not forgetting—it is failing to act once the patient knows they cannot come.

Run this four-week test before buying new software:

```text
Hypothesis: Requiring a 7-day keep/change response plus a 48-hour personal chase
will reduce unfilled hygiene no-shows from 14% to under 8%.

Critical assumption tested: Nonattendance is materially recoverable through earlier
patient decisions, not mainly unavoidable same-day events.

Experiment: Randomly assign three hygiene schedules to the new workflow and keep
three on the current 48-hour SMS-only workflow for four weeks. Use the same
release-and-waitlist process on the test schedules.

Resources: One coordinator; a simple tracking sheet; 10–15 minutes per day;
existing texting/phone tools.

Measured outcome: Per schedule, record attended appointments, no-shows, early
releases, and early releases successfully refilled.

Success threshold: Test schedules produce at least a 40% lower unfilled-loss rate
than control, without more than 15 minutes of daily coordinator work.

Failure threshold: Less than a 15% reduction, or fewer than 20% of early releases
are refilled.

Learned either way: Whether the binding problem is patient commitment or the
practice’s ability to refill capacity.

If validated: Roll out the workflow, then introduce recurring-slot priority for
reliable patients.

If falsified: Interview 15 recent no-show patients using a nonjudgmental script
to identify the dominant barrier—cost, transport, work, anxiety, illness, or
simple disengagement—and redesign for that barrier.
```

Kill list:

- “Send reminders sooner and more often” — likely message fatigue; it does not create a decision or refill opportunity.
- Blanket no-show fees — may collect money but can damage trust, reduce return visits, and do little to recover chair time.
- Routine overbooking — can trade empty chairs for late care and stressed clinicians.
- Generic AI prediction — useful later, but it does not solve the workflow unless different-risk patients receive a different offer.
- One universal policy — ignores the distinction between reliable recurring patients and repeat late cancellers.

What may still be missing: the practice should examine whether the 14% clusters by hygienist, appointment time, patient cohort, insurance type, booking lead time, or particular recall interval. If it clusters, a practice-wide reminder change is too blunt; redesign the specific failure pattern instead.
